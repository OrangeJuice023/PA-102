# Topic 2.1 to 2.2: Constitutional design and mandates

> [!plain] In plain words
> the Constitution is the highest law, so anything against it is void. Power is split among 3 branches that check each other. Government does 4 kinds of work, and laws set what officials must do and how they answer for it.

## 2.1 Formal institutions of the State

**Readings:** Const. Art. II, Sec. 1; Arts. VI, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, XII, XIII, XVI; Admin Code Sec. 2, Bk. I Ch. 9, Bk. II, Bk. IV; **P.D. No. 807** (Civil Service Decree); **Feliciano**, "Government and the State."

- **Feliciano:** lays out the whole structure of the State and government: its power, functions, and control.
- **The 3 branches:** **Art. VI = Legislative, Art. VII = Executive, Art. VIII = Judicial.**
- Beyond the 3 branches are the **independent bodies** (Constitutional Commissions, Ombudsman). They protect the integrity of government and people, like a "fourth branch" (Galindez compares this to Australia).
- **Sir's (LTD's) own way of ranking institutions:** by **power**, **function**, and **independent bodies**.
- **Administrative State** = the State intervenes in the economy.
- **Quasi-legislative power:** Congress is slow, so agencies issue rules (election, human rights, civil service rules) to make things move faster.

## Constitutional supremacy and hierarchy of laws

**Readings:** Civil Code Art. 7; Manila Prince Hotel; Tañada; Sameer Overseas.

- The Constitution is the **[[Constitutional Supremacy|supreme law]]** that sets the boundaries of public power. Hierarchy: **Constitution → statutes → administrative rules/orders.**
- **4 keywords (Sir's):**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Supreme** | Highest law of the land |
| **Imperious** | Must be taken seriously |
| **Absolute** | Applies to all branches |
| **Unalterable** | No ordinary law can go against it |

- A law that **violates** the Constitution is **null and void**. If a law is **partly** valid and partly invalid, only the valid part applies.

| Case | What to remember |
| --- | --- |
| **Manila Prince Hotel v. GSIS** (G.R. No. 122156, Feb. 3, 1997) | The **[[Filipino First Policy\|Filipino First policy]]** (Art. XII, Sec. 10) is **self-executing**. **Self-executing** = complete in itself, courts can enforce it without a new law. **Non-self-executing** = general principles or state policies that need an act of Congress (enabling law) before they can be enforced. |
| **Tañada v. Angara** (G.R. No. 118295, May 2, 1997) | Read the Constitution **as a whole**, because some provisions are non-self-executing. |
| **Sameer Overseas v. Cabiles** (G.R. No. 170139, Aug. 5, 2014) | A clause of **RA 8042** was declared unconstitutional, then **RA 10022 (March 8, 2010)** reinstated it. The SC ruled it **still invalid**. An unconstitutional law **"confers no rights; it imposes no duties; it affords no protection; it creates no office; it is inoperative as if it has not been passed at all."** Also: RA 10022 was not yet in effect when the worker was fired in 1997, so the old RA 8042 governed. |

## Separation of powers and checks and balances

| Case | What to remember |
| --- | --- |
| **Angara v. Electoral Commission** (G.R. No. L-45081, July 15, 1936) | Branches are **independent and co-equal**. [[Separation of Powers\|Separation of powers]] is **not an express provision**; it comes from how the Constitution divides powers into **Arts. VI, VII, VIII**. |
| **Macalintal v. COMELEC** (G.R. No. 157013, July 10, 2003), **Puno, J.** (concurring and dissenting) | Separation of powers **prevents concentrating** legislative, executive, and judicial power in one branch. Dates back to **Aristotle**; the modern idea comes from **Locke and Montesquieu**, reacting to power struggles between monarchs and parliaments in Western Europe. |
| **Planas v. Gil** (67 Phil. 62, 1939), citing **Holmes, J.** in Springer v. Government of the PI | **Interdependence** is more real than strict independence. Branches can't be divided "with mathematical precision" into **"watertight compartments"**; the Constitution doesn't divide "fields of black and white." |

**Key idea:** separation of powers is **not watertight**. Each branch is supreme in its own sphere, but the others can check it.

## 4 kinds of public power or functions

**Readings:** Bacani v. NACOCO (1956); ACCFA v. CUGCO (1969); Republic v. City of Davao (2002).

**Constituent v. ministrant:**

|  | Constituent | Ministrant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | The **very bonds of society** | Advance the **general interests** of society |
| Nature | **Compulsory** | **Optional**; support functions the private sector can also manage |
| Examples | See the 8 below | Public works, public education, public charity, health and safety regulations, regulation of trade and industry. Galindez adds inventions and scientific discovery. |

**The 8 constituent functions:**

1. Keeping order; protecting persons and property from violence and robbery.
2. Fixing legal relations between husband and wife, and between parents and children.
3. Regulating the holding, transmission, and exchange of property, and its liabilities for debt or crime.
4. Determining contract rights between individuals.
5. Defining and punishing crime.
6. Administering justice in civil cases.
7. Determining the political duties, privileges, and relations of citizens.
8. Dealing with foreign powers: protecting the state from outside danger and advancing its international interests.

**Governmental v. proprietary:**

|  | Governmental | Proprietary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Administering state powers and promoting public welfare: legislative, judicial, public, and political powers. **Exercise of public office.** | For the **special benefit** of the community: ministerial, private, and corporate powers. Government acts as an **economic entity**. |
| Liability | Generally **immune from suit** for agents' negligence, **unless waived**. **Presumption of regularity**: to remove someone from office you must prove liability. | Government **strips away its sovereign mantle** and **can be held liable** like any private company or person. |
| Examples | Delivery of sand for a municipal road, local legislation, control over police, abatement of nuisance | Public cemeteries |

> Not in the notes, please verify: ACCFA v. CUGCO (1969) is often cited for the point that the constituent/ministrant line has blurred, since a modern welfare state takes on more ministrant work.

## Mandates of and on public power

**General mandates:**

- **Art. II:** Declaration of Principles and State Policies. **Sec. 1:** democratic and republican state; [[Sovereignty|sovereignty]] resides in the people.
- **Art. III:** **Bill of Rights.** Examples: no cruel or inhuman punishment; **no imprisonment for debt** (unless there is criminal liability; otherwise it's a civil case).
- **Art. XI, Sec. 1:** **Public office is a public trust.** Art. XI sets accountability for the President, VP, SC justices, Constitutional Commission members, and the Ombudsman.
- **Calalang v. Williams** (1940) is in the syllabus here, but neither set of notes covers it.

> [!warning] Check
> your notes say Art. III is about **citizenship** under the **1973** Constitution. For the **1987** Constitution, **Art. III = Bill of Rights** and **Art. IV = Citizenship**. Galindez's notes match this.

**Public finance mandates:** Constitution; Admin Code Bk. VI; **State Audit Code (1978)**. These govern government spending and public money.

**Civil service mandates:** Const. **Art. IX-B**; Admin Code Bk. I, Ch. 9.

**Policymaking mandates:** Cases: **Luzon Stevedoring v. SSS** (1966), **Octaviano v. Board of Architecture** (2023), **MERALCO v. City of Muntinlupa** (2021).

- **Conclusion 1:** administrative rule-making must **align with the delegation** (the law that granted the power).
- **Conclusion 2 (MERALCO):** **"You cannot rise above your source."** Codes set the limits of what you can do.

**Accountability mandates:**

| Law | Name |
| --- | --- |
| RA 1379 (1955) | Forfeiture Law |
| RA 3019 (1960) | Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act |
| RA 6713 (1989) | Code of Conduct and Ethical Standards |
| RA 7080 (1991) | Plunder Law |
| RA 9160 (2001) | Anti-Money Laundering Act |

These hold officials accountable for misusing public funds or using their position for personal gain.

## How the 1987 Constitution responded to Marcos (Galindez)

- **Expanded [[Judicial Review|judicial review]]:** courts can review **any branch** for **grave abuse of discretion**.
- **[[Martial Law|Martial law]] time limit:** **60 days**; any extension is subject to review and approval.

> [!warning] Check
> Galindez cites these as "Sec. 8, Art. 2, par. 2" and "Sec. 18, Art. 8." The standard cites are **Art. VIII, Sec. 1(2)** (judicial review) and **Art. VII, Sec. 18** (martial law).

## Core idea

**Formal institutions + [[Informal Institutions|informal institutions]] → actual behavior → governance.** Example: procurement has rules (formal), but "may kilala ako sa loob" (informal) shapes what really happens.
