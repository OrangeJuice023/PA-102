# Topic 3.5: The birth of the mestizo (Agabin)

> [!verify] \[NEEDS VERIFICATION\]

> [!plain] In plain words
> our legal system is a mix. Spain gave us the civil codes for family, property, and contracts. America added the Constitution, [[Police Power|police power]], [[Due Process|due process]], court procedure, and commercial law. That blend is what Agabin calls "[[Mestizo Legal System|mestizo]]." It's about legal mixing, not race.

**Main thesis:** **Spanish civil law + American common law + local/customary law = mestizo legal system.**

## Historical background

- **Feb. 15, 1898:** USS Maine explodes in Havana → catalyst for the Spanish-American War (April 1898).
- **May 1, 1898:** Battle of Manila Bay; **Admiral George Dewey** defeats the Spanish fleet.
- **Manifest Destiny:** American expansionism framed as civilization, education, uplift, Christianization.
- **Dec. 10, 1898:** **Treaty of Paris**: Spain cedes the PH to the US for **$20 million**.
- **Feb. 1899:** Philippine-American War.
- The **US War Department** (Secretary **Elihu Root**) ran PH affairs.
- **Sir's point:** the takeover was not just military; it became a **legal and institutional transformation**.

## 3 levels of American penetration

1. **Political:** occupation → annexation → colonial government.
2. **Institutional:** reorganized government, courts, procedure, legislation, administration.
3. **Jurisprudential:** reshaped due process, police power, liberty of contract, precedent, judicial interpretation.

**Conquest → institutionalization → legal hybridization.**

## What Spain left vs. what America added (Sir's lecture)

| Spanish changes | American changes |
| --- | --- |
| **Código Civil:** property and economics (private property); family and faith | **Politics → constitutionalism:** the Constitution as [[Constitutional Supremacy\|supreme law]]: **imperious, absolute, unalterable, supreme** |
| **Código de Comercio** | **Control:** the hegemony of **police power** and regulation |
| **Código Penal:** accountability and liability | **Property:** no monarch, so who owns everything? **The State.** Plus **self-protection** (title; right to protect your property from intrusion) and **social function** (freedom, but not at the expense of others) |
| Law is still mostly understood the way Spain understood it | **Due process:** violence is no longer private; the **State** uses it through the courts |

- **Fun fact:** the **easement of light and view** lets you act against a structure that blocks your window's light.

## Constitutionalism and the colonial contradiction

- Americans said Filipinos needed to be civilized, educated, and prepared for self-government by American constitutional standards (rule of law, liberty, good government).
- **Contradiction:** teaching constitutional government while keeping colonial [[Sovereignty|sovereignty]].
- **Constitutional supremacy:** Constitution → statutes → administrative rules. A lower law can't override the Constitution. **Government itself is subject to law.**
- **Does the US Constitution "follow the flag"?** **Not automatically, and not entirely** (the **Insular Cases**): the PH was an **[[Unincorporated Territory|unincorporated territory]]**. The **Organic Act of 1902** made inhabitants **"citizens of the Philippine Islands,"** not US citizens. This affected constitutional rights, taxation, political rights.
- **Policy of attraction:** **Taft** used **ilustrados** (lawyers, doctors, professionals, landowners) as intermediaries. **Partido Federalista** favored closer US integration, even statehood.
- The **US Supreme Court** had appellate jurisdiction over PH cases, so PH courts relied on **American precedents**. The 1900 **Philippine Commission** had legislative and executive powers; Americans dominated the judiciary.

## Police power and property

- **Police power** = the State's power to regulate private conduct, property, and business for **public welfare and self-protection**. Three ideas: **self-protection, state authority, social function.**
- **Private rights are not absolute.** Property can be regulated when its use harms others, affects public welfare, creates a nuisance, or threatens safety.
- **Property → social function → police power → regulation.**
- **De Villata v. Stanley:** shipping companies were required to carry government mail. The Court first upheld it (entering a regulated business means accepting conditions); the **US Supreme Court reversed** it under due process. Shows the tension between **police power** and **due process / property rights**.

## Due process

| Procedural due process | Substantive due process |
| --- | --- |
| Was the correct **procedure** followed? | Is the government **action itself** allowed? Even with proper procedure, it can be unconstitutional if it interferes too much with liberty or property. |

**Development:** early period → police power emphasized → **1920s** → substantive due process → liberty of contract → laissez-faire → stronger protection of property and economic liberty.

## Procedure: inquisitorial → adversarial

| Spanish: inquisitorial | American: accusatorial / adversarial |
| --- | --- |
| The court has a strong investigative role | Prosecution and defense argue before a **neutral** court |

- **General Order No. 68:** ended exclusive **church control over marriage**; recognized **civil marriage** (connected to the old Siete Partidas framework).
- **General Order No. 58 (April 23, 1900):** new **Code of Criminal Procedure**; **inquisitorial → accusatorial**; aimed to protect civil liberties.
- The **Philippine Commission** passed a large body of public law: municipal and provincial government, Manila, elections, Baguio, Mindanao and Sulu, courts, civil procedure.

## Jurisprudence, equity, and common law

- **Jurisprudence = juris** (law, rights) + **prudence** (careful judgment). Court → decision → legal reasoning → jurisprudence → **precedent** → **binding effect**. A decision can have the **"force and effect of law."** Common law is not "judges making random laws."

| Courts of law | Courts of equity |
| --- | --- |
| Apply established legal rules | Find the **appropriate solution with respect to the rights involved**: fairness, remedies, balancing rights |

Over time, law and equity **merged**. (Galindez: common law used to be just courts of equity; when merged with courts of law, law and rights were combined.)

| Civil law | Common law |
| --- | --- |
| Codes and statutes from parliament/legislature | Judicial decisions and precedent from courts |
| Spanish foundation | Anglo-American influence |

We are a **mix of both**, and the two can say completely different things.

## Illegally obtained evidence

**Spanish:** generally admissible → **early American** (Adams v. New York tradition): still admissible → later PH developments → eventually the **exclusionary rule**. Americanization happened in steps, not all at once.

## Retention of private and customary law

- Americanization did **not** replace everything. The **Spanish Civil Code of 1889** stayed important for family, property, contracts, succession, personal status.
- The **Spanish Penal Code of 1870** stayed foundational and became the basis of the **Revised Penal Code**.
- **Customary law** continued where state penetration was incomplete: **Muslim Mindanao, highland communities, ancestral domains**.

## American common-law doctrines inserted into Spanish law

| Area | Spanish base | American additions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Torts / quasi-delicts** | Spanish structure | Proximate cause, contributory negligence, last clear chance, vicarious liability, sovereign immunity |
| **Contracts** | **Causa** | **Consideration** (both now part of PH contract law) |
| **Property** | Movable / immovable | Land / personal property, equity, trusts |
| **Succession** | Spanish framework | Heir, devisee, legatee |

**Commercial law = the heaviest Americanization:** Corporation Law (Act No. 1459), Negotiable Instruments Law (Act No. 2031), Insolvency Act (Act No. 1956), Warehouse Receipts Law (Act No. 2137), Insurance Law (Act No. 2427), public utilities laws.

## The mestizo legal system

| Spanish foundation | American overlay | Local/customary layer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civil law, family, property, contracts, succession, much criminal law | Constitutional and public law, [[Judicial Review\|judicial review]], procedure, commercial law, precedent, due process, police power, common-law reasoning | Survived where state penetration was incomplete |

**"Mestizo" is a metaphor for legal hybridization**, not racial mixing.
