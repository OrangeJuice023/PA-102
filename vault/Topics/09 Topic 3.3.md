> [!plain] In plain words
> the US built the PH state through a civil service, English-language schools, and a military police, and slowly let Filipinos take part. But the US kept [[Sovereignty|sovereignty]]. This is where our militarized governance and copied American system come from.

## Legal status (Galindez)

- The PH was an **unincorporated territory** of the US.
- The **First Philippine Republic never had sovereignty**; it was a **belligerent [[De Facto Government|de facto]]** government. Sovereignty passed from **Spain to the US**, so the US represented the PH internationally and replaced the de facto Philippine government.
- The Americans removed the powers Filipinos had over local governments and replaced them.

## McKinley's orders (Galindez)

1. Establish order and municipal government.
2. Build the state and conquer the rest of the territory (Mindanao).
3. Govern for the happiness of Filipinos, **even conforming to their prejudices** (Christian vs. non-Christian), so government could discriminate against non-Christians.

## State-building: McKinley's 3 priorities (Abinales)

1. **Bureau of Civil Service:** the **first bureau** created. "Greatest care should be taken in the selection of officials." Heads were American civilians or honorably discharged soldiers, "men of the highest character and fitness," free from partisan politics. Two goals: (a) be different from Spanish rule; (b) politics must not undermine the [[Bureaucracy (Weber)|bureaucracy]]. The civil service was **"a regime of law, not of men."** First run by military men and American civilians; few Filipinos in executive posts.
2. **Public education:** free elementary and secondary schooling, because a democracy needs literate citizens. **English** as the common language, since Filipinos had no unified language. The first English-speaking Filipinos became teachers. Spain had avoided teaching Spanish widely for fear it would give Filipinos too much **cohesion** and lead to uprisings. Tagalog was considered (newspapers used it), but the Americans **vetoed vernaculars** until the end of colonial rule.
3. **[[Philippine Constabulary]] (1901 to 1991):** a **gendarmerie** (military doing law enforcement) replacing the insular police. Regulated firearms, handled **health emergencies** (no health department yet), expanded telegraph and postal systems, suppressed brigandage and insurrection, supervised local police.

- **Sir's point:** the Constabulary is why PH governance is **militarized**: military and police handle disasters and health crises, even the pandemic. The US wanted to demilitarize colonies but keep a security force. It was raised to **suppress its own people**, which shaped soldiers' attitudes during martial law.
- **Failures:** **land policy** (land reform without support; farmers went back to tenancy) and **tax policy** (one peso for a poor peasant, only 35 pesos for a rich landowner).

## Special provinces (Mindanao, Cordillera)

- Areas Spain barely colonized, mostly non-Christian. Both were held under **military control**; some local leaders saw the US as protection for their trade from Filipino control.
- **Two governments (Galindez):** the main Government of the PH and the special **Moro Province** under a military governor. The Americans had no prior experience with Islam. The Moro Province was never integrated; local leaders stayed under American control.
- **Bureau of Non-Christian Tribes:** shifted from religious to **ethno-racial** categories; said **"Filipino" applied only to Christians**. Non-Christians were still seen as uncivilized.
- Constabulary officers thought integration was **two generations away**. The army eventually handed power to civilian leaders in Manila.
- **Legacy:** non-Christians became "minority outsiders," and the **American-drawn map** shaped the Bangsamoro territory Islamic movements fight for.
- The **Sultan of Sulu** surrendered sovereignty through a **memorandum agreement** with the US (Galindez).

## Organic laws and structure

| Law | Year | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Philippine Bill (Cooper Act)** | **July 1, 1902** | Basic law for **14 years**; a **transitional constitution** built like a constitution. Had a **bill of rights** (speech, religion). **Sovereignty followed the US flag, but not the Constitution.** Local courts (Courts of First Instance) but **no Court of Appeals**: the **US Supreme Court** was the final court, and the PH Supreme Court acted like an appeals court. |
| **Jones Law** | **Aug. 29, 1916** | The upper house (Philippine Commission) became the **Philippine Senate**, with a **Senate President**: the first local use of "President." |
| **Commonwealth** | **1935** | First government of our own: President **Manuel Quezon**, VP, cabinet |

- **Structure under the Philippine Bill (Galindez):** US President → Governor → Executive Bureau, alongside the Philippine Assembly and Supreme Court. Later the Commission was the upper house and the Assembly the lower house.
- **Galindez's summary:** 1902, everything dictated by the US (Cooper Act) → 1916, Senate and Senate President → Commonwealth.
- **Senate history (Galindez):** the National Assembly (1935) became bicameral (1940). Senators were first elected by **senatorial districts** (drawn arbitrarily), later dissolved; districts became administrative regions. After **1973**, senators no longer represented regions but served as a **national check** guarding democracy against the executive.

## Malcolm: rise of constitutional thought

- Filipinos absorbed **Latin** (Spanish) culture and the **American** idea of good government.
- Filipinos showed their aptitude for self-government through the **Biak-na-Bato** and **[[Malolos Constitution|Malolos]]** constitutions.
- **3 most important organic laws:** McKinley's Instructions (April 7, 1900), the Philippine Bill (July 1, 1902), the Jones Law (Aug. 29, 1916).

## Conservative nationalism

- Elites knew the war was lost, so they accepted the American offer to help govern, to keep wealth and status (they had privilege under late Spanish rule, and the US system favored them).
- **Partido Federal (1900):** true to the revolution's spirit but realistic about US power. Foundation of **conservative nationalism**.
- **Nacionalista Party:** independence under US protection: accept US rule, increase Filipino autonomy, convince the Americans, and reduce American interest.
- Armed resistance continued; independence stayed popular among poorer, less-educated classes.
- **Continuing revolutionary tradition:** Malolos unity = the coming together of men of wealth, education, and social prominence, with no concept of **loob**. Katipunan = loob (love, compassion, virtue). Early elections and public schools **neutralized** lower-class movements.

## Foreign and Filipino thought

- **Coolidge's reply to Roxas:** no independence yet: (1) not ready in wealth; (2) the Commission didn't reflect Filipino sentiment.
- **Woodrow Wilson:** Filipinos were "immature" and needed guidance before self-government.
- **Taft-era observers:** some favorable, some critical.
- **Sofia de Veyra:** women in government, medicine (UP School of Hygiene), and public health.
- **Rafael Palma** (in your notes as a reading).
- **Sir's counterpoint:** Filipino women already reached high positions despite American limits. **So who is really immature?**
- Filipinos were **divided** on what they wanted from independence.

## Big idea of 3.3

American rule built institutions and let Filipinos participate, but **kept sovereignty**. Filipino ideas of governance have deep roots in American colonialism and in copying the American system.
