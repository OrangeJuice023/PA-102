# 4.2 The Legislative and Judicial Bureaucracies

## Overview

The legislative and judicial [[Bureaucracy (Weber)|bureaucracies]] are the internal organizations that allow Congress and the courts to perform their constitutional functions. It is useful to distinguish the elected or constitutionally appointed decision-makers from the permanent administrative personnel and technical offices that support them. For example, senators exercise legislative authority, while the Senate Secretariat provides continuing administrative, research, legal, financial, and constituent-service support.

The legislative branch primarily makes laws and performs oversight. The Judiciary resolves actual legal controversies, interprets and applies the law, and reviews government action for grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction.

## A. Legislative bureaucracy

### Constitutional source and plenary legislative power

**1987 Constitution, Article VI, Section 1** vests legislative power in the Congress of the Philippines, composed of the Senate and the House of Representatives, except to the extent that the people reserve legislative power through initiative and referendum.

The November 7 and in-class notes describe legislative power as **plenary**: broad, general, and comprehensive. The point is that Congress may legislate on a wide range of public matters, but its authority remains constitutionally limited. Plenary does not mean unlimited or immune from [[Judicial Review|judicial review]].

The lecture also connects legislative power to the State's three inherent powers:

- **[[Police Power|Police power]]:** regulation for public welfare, health, safety, morals, and general welfare.
- **Power of eminent domain:** taking of private property for public use, subject to just compensation.
- **Power of taxation:** raising revenue to support government and public purposes.

These are related to legislative authority but are not interchangeable. Congress may enact laws exercising or implementing these powers, while executive agencies may exercise delegated regulatory authority within statutory and constitutional limits.

### Internal powers of each House

**Article VI, Section 16(2)–(5)** governs important internal matters:

- **Quorum:** a majority of each House constitutes a quorum to do business. A smaller number may adjourn from day to day and compel attendance under its rules.
- **Discipline:** each House may punish disorderly members. Suspension or expulsion requires a two-thirds vote of all members of the House concerned. A suspension may not exceed 60 days.
- **Journal and records:** each House must keep and periodically publish a Journal, subject to the national-security exception. At the request of one-fifth of members present, the yeas and nays must be recorded.
- **Adjournment:** neither House may adjourn for more than three days or move to another place during a session without the consent of the other House.

The key distinction is that each House controls its internal proceedings, but this autonomy operates within constitutional limits.

### Senate and House leadership

The Second Year notes list each House's principal officers "based on" that House's own rules:

- **Senate:** Senate President, Senate President Pro Tempore, Senate Secretary, Sergeant-at-Arms.
- **House of Representatives:** Speaker of the House, Deputy Speaker, Secretary General, Sergeant-at-Arms.

The lecture adds a practical quorum note: the Senate cannot vote without a quorum, but senators can still meet to discuss or deliberate on topics.

### Legislative procedure and constitutional checks

**Initiative vs. referendum:** Initiative begins with a proposal by the people. Referendum asks voters to approve or reject a law or part of a law. The constitutional petition requirements include signatures of at least 10% of all registered voters nationwide, with at least 3% of registered voters in every legislative district, subject to implementing law and applicable exceptions. The President's side of legislation — veto, line-item veto, and budget control — is detailed in Sir's own words in [[15 OCT 3 Raw Notes|OCT 3 - Raw Notes]].

### Power of the purse (Sections 25 and 29)

The Galindez notes head this discussion "Power of the Purse" (Art. VI, Secs. 25 and 29). The constitutional rules restrict even Congress itself:

- **Section 25(1):** Congress may not increase the appropriations recommended by the President for the operation of the Government as specified in the budget. The form, content, and manner of preparing the budget are prescribed by law.
- **Section 25(2):** Congress must follow the same procedure for appropriations for Congress itself as for other departments and agencies.
- **Section 25(3):** Congress may not enact a law authorizing transfers of appropriations. By law, however, the President, Senate President, Speaker of the House, Chief Justice, and heads of Constitutional Commissions may augment an item in their own appropriations from savings in other items.
- **Section 25(4):** A special appropriations bill must state its purpose and be supported by funds actually available (certified by the National Treasurer) or a corresponding revenue proposal.
- **Section 25(5):** No law may authorize a transfer of appropriations, except the augmentation-from-savings exception. This is the provision the Court applied in Macalintal v. COMELEC below.
- **Section 25(6):** Discretionary funds for particular officials must be used for public purposes, supported by vouchers, and governed by law.
- **Section 25(7):** If Congress fails to pass the next General Appropriations Bill, the previous year's General Appropriations Act is deemed reenacted.
- **Section 29(1):** No money may be paid out of the Treasury except by an appropriation made by law.
- **Section 29(2)–(3):** Public money generally cannot support religious groups or leaders (with narrow exceptions such as chaplains in the military, penal institutions, orphanages, or leprosaria); special-purpose tax proceeds may be used only for that purpose.

### Oversight, inquiries, and accountability

- **Article VI, Section 21:** Congress or its committees may conduct inquiries in aid of legislation, in accordance with duly published rules and with respect for the rights of persons appearing or affected.
- **Article VI, Section 22:** department heads may appear before either House on their own initiative with the President's consent, or upon the request of either House, on matters pertaining to their departments. Questions must relate to their departments and are recorded in the Journal. Written questions must be submitted to the Senate President or the Speaker at least three days before the scheduled appearance, but interpellations are not limited to the written questions and may cover related matters. When the security of the State or the public interest so requires and the President states so in writing, the appearance is conducted in executive session.
- The notes identify legislation, oversight, appointments through the Commission on Appointments, impeachment initiation, budget approval, action on [[Martial Law|martial law]] and [[Emergency Powers|emergency powers]], declaration of war, and Senate concurrence in treaties as important congressional responsibilities.

**Arnault v. Nazareno:** The Supreme Court recognized that the power to conduct inquiries in aid of legislation includes the authority to require relevant testimony and to punish contempt. A witness cannot avoid a pertinent question through an unsupported claim of self-incrimination. The contempt power may continue beyond the adjournment of the session in the circumstances recognized by the Court.

**Macalintal v. COMELEC, G.R. Nos. 263590 and 263673 (June 27, 2023):** Congress has broad authority to legislate on election schedules, including postponement, but the power is not absolute. The Court struck down RA 11935, which postponed the 2022 Barangay and Sangguniang Kabataan elections, because the measure unjustifiably impaired suffrage and substantive [[Due Process|due process]] and was tied to an impermissible budget rationale. The budget rationale failed because Article VI, Section 25(5) restricts transfers of appropriations: the realigned COMELEC election budget could not simply be diverted to Executive programs. The Court discussed the operative fact doctrine and clarified that hold-over tenure is distinct from the legally fixed term of office: **term of office** is the period fixed by law, while **tenure** is the period the official actually occupies the office, so hold-over service extends tenure but not the term.

**ABAKADA GURO Partylist v. Purisima, G.R. No. 166715 (August 14, 2008):** mentioned in the class notes under "Legislative Veto and Procedure in Lawmaking." The notes give no doctrine or digest for this case.

### Internal administrative structure of Congress

The class notes distinguish the **Senate Proper** from the **Senate Secretariat**:

- **Senate Proper:** elected senators and their co-terminous personal staff.
- **Senate Secretariat:** regular institutional employees who provide continuing administrative and technical support.

The notes identify the following Senate support offices and functions:

- **Office of the Senate Secretary:** supports the Senate President and senators and oversees the Secretariat. The in-class notes add that the Secretary is elected and works through deputy secretaries.
- **Blue Ribbon Oversight Office Management / BROOM:** technical and administrative support connected with oversight work. The notes say it was created by Policy Order 2004-001.
- **Senate Tax Study and Research Office (STSRO):** technical assistance on tax proposals.
- **Senate Economic Planning Office (SEPO):** research and economic-policy support. The notes say it was created by Senate Resolution No. 65.
- **Legislative Budget Research and Monitoring Office (LBRMO):** budgetary and fiscal research.
- **Office of the Senate Legal Counsel (OSLC):** legal assistance to senators.
- **Senate Public Assistance Office (SPAO):** helps constituents obtain referrals to relevant government agencies.
- **EDP-MIS (Electronic Data Processing and Management Information System Bureau):** information technology — tech and information systems.
- **PRIB (Public Relations and Information Bureau):** disseminating legal information, including legislative information to media platforms.
- **MPOAB:** mentioned in the notes for management planning; no further detail is given.

The notes group the Secretariat's main operating arms as **Departments of the Senate**, each headed by a Deputy Secretary except as noted:

- **Legislation:** legislative services in support of plenary affairs, committee affairs, research, and reference.
- **Administrative and Financial Services:** formulates and implements the Senate's administrative and financial policies and programs.
- **External Affairs and Relations:** networking and interaction with the academe, private sector, NGOs, and national and international organizations; advises and assists the Senate Secretary on external-affairs policies and programs.
- **Office of the Sergeant-at-Arms:** security, peace, and order within the Senate; its head is elected by the Senators.

**Office of International Relations and Protocol (OIRP):** the notes call it the institutional bridge supporting the Senate President and senators in international engagement, parliamentary diplomacy, and protocol. It has two services:

- **International Relations Service** — policy, research, and parliamentary engagement: coordinates with the DFA, government agencies, and diplomatic missions; research and briefing; speeches, opening statements, resolutions, and talking points; inter-parliamentary assemblies and conferences; meeting notes, highlights, and photo captions.
- **Protocol Service** — official visits, travel, and ceremonial coordination: travel authority and documents (passport and visa assistance, airport arrival and departure support, end-to-end travel coordination); visits and conferences (advance teams, official functions, embassy and consulate coordination, venue and seating, transportation, appreciation dinners, invitations, tokens and protocol requirements).

The lecture frames this as **parliamentary diplomacy**: the Senate advances PH interests through legislation, international engagement, and institutional support, connecting PH perspectives to multilateral dialogue, legislative exchange, and shared regional priorities (including the Inter-Parliamentary Union).

## B. Judicial bureaucracy

### Constitutional mandate

**Article VIII, Section 1** vests judicial power in one Supreme Court and in lower courts established by law. Judicial power includes:

1. Settling actual controversies involving rights that are legally demandable and enforceable; and
2. Determining whether any branch or instrumentality of government committed grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction.

This second component is significant because judicial review is not limited to ordinary disputes between private parties. Courts may review government action when the constitutional standard is met.

### Structure of the Judiciary

- **Supreme Court:** the highest judicial body — one Chief Justice and 14 Associate Justices. It interprets the Constitution and laws, reviews qualifying lower-court decisions, checks grave abuse of discretion, supervises the Judiciary, and promulgates procedural rules. Sir stresses that the Supreme Court does **not** conduct trials; it reviews decisions of lower courts, and decides cases directly only for controversies that are legally demandable and enforceable, such as constitutional cases.
- **Court of Appeals:** the notes draw the key distinction between **adjudicative functions** (resolving cases and issuing decisions) and **administrative functions** (case processing, records, finance, personnel, technology, and security) that let the Court operate.
- **Court of Tax Appeals:** a specialized collegiate court for tax disputes, with its own judicial and administrative structure — Presiding Justice and Associate Justices; Clerk of Court and Division Clerks; Office of Legal and Technical Services (research and analysis); Office of Administrative and Finance Services (personnel, finances, facilities); and an Internal Audit Service.
- **Sandiganbayan:** Sir calls it the "weird" one out — classified as a collegiate court but functioning like both a trial and an appellate court. It is the people's court for citizens' grievances against public officials: a special court for specified graft and corruption cases, recognized under both the 1973 and 1987 Constitutions, with jurisdiction set by the applicable laws, the offense, and the accused's position or rank. Guiding principle: public office is a public trust.
- **Trial courts:** where cases normally begin and factual issues are first resolved (see the hierarchy discussion below). The in-class notes add the working vocabulary: a **branch** or **sala** is a trial courtroom with a judge, and the **chamber** is the judge's private office.

### Judicial independence and administration

Important provisions include:

- **Section 2:** Congress may define and distribute the jurisdiction of courts, but cannot deprive the Supreme Court of constitutionally granted jurisdiction or undermine the security of tenure of members of the Judiciary.
- **Section 3:** the Judiciary enjoys fiscal autonomy. Its appropriations may not be reduced below the previous year's appropriation, and approved appropriations must be automatically and regularly released.
- **Section 4(2)–(3):** cases involving the constitutionality of a treaty, international or executive agreement, or law must be heard and decided by the Court **en banc**, as must other cases the Rules of Court require to be heard en banc (including those involving presidential decrees, proclamations, orders, instructions, ordinances, and regulations). A case heard by a division may be referred to the full Court when a majority of the division's members recommends it, under the applicable constitutional and procedural rules.
- **Section 5:** the Supreme Court's powers include **original jurisdiction** (ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls; petitions for certiorari, prohibition, mandamus, quo warranto, and habeas corpus); **appellate jurisdiction** (final judgments and orders of lower courts in cases provided by the Constitution and law, including constitutionality, jurisdiction, and questions of law); **temporary assignment** of lower-court judges to other stations when public interest requires, within the constitutional time limit; **change of venue** to avoid a miscarriage of justice; **rule-making** (constitutional rights, pleading, practice, procedure, admission to the Bar, the Integrated Bar, legal assistance to the underprivileged); and **appointment** of all Judiciary officials and employees under the Civil Service Law.
- **Section 6:** the Supreme Court exercises administrative supervision over all courts and court personnel.
- **Sections 13–16:** require proper certification and explanation of decisions, impose time limits for deciding cases, and require an annual report on Judiciary operations.

### Questions of fact, law, and policy

The in-class notes draw the distinction in the lecture's own words: the trier of facts handles the question of fact, while the trier of laws handles the question of law.

- **Question of fact:** whether something actually happened.
- **Question of law:** the application of specific laws to a specific event.
- **Question of policy (political question):** depends on the justification behind a policy and the political decision made, so it requires the executive and is not under the jurisdiction of the Judiciary.

### Hierarchy of courts and special tribunals

The hierarchy of courts is not merely a matter of rank. It guides where a case should normally begin and which court should resolve factual issues in the first instance.

**GIOS-SAMAR, Inc. v. DOTC, G.R. No. 217158 (March 12, 2019):**

- The petition was filed directly with the Supreme Court and challenged the bundling of airport public-private partnership projects.
- The Court dismissed the petition, emphasizing that parties must generally observe the hierarchy of courts even where the Supreme Court shares original jurisdiction with lower courts.
- Invoking transcendental importance does not automatically justify bypassing lower courts, especially where factual evidence is needed.
- The Court also explained that an exclusive public-service concession does not automatically constitute an unconstitutional monopoly; the claim must be supported by sufficient facts.

The notes also identify several special adjudicatory bodies:

- **Presidential Electoral Tribunal (PET):** the Supreme Court sitting as the tribunal for election contests involving the President and Vice-President.
- **Senate Electoral Tribunal (SET) and House of Representatives Electoral Tribunal (HRET):** constitutional tribunals with exclusive authority over contests involving the election, returns, and qualifications of their respective members.
- **Impeachment Court:** the Senate exercising its constitutional role in the trial of impeachment cases. It is not an ordinary criminal court. Impeachment is also summarized in [[14b Topic 4.3|Topic 4.3]], and a vote-count example appears in [[12 Topic 3.6|Topic 3.6]].

**Macalintal v. PET:** The notes cite the doctrine that resolving presidential election contests is a traditional judicial function, so Supreme Court Justices sitting as the PET do not violate the constitutional restriction on non-judicial assignments.

## Key takeaways for 4.2

- Congress has broad legislative power, but every exercise remains subject to constitutional limits.
- Legislative support offices are part of the bureaucracy that allows Congress to perform its functions.
- Congressional inquiries must be in aid of legislation and must respect individual rights.
- Judicial power includes review for grave abuse of discretion.
- Judicial independence is supported by security of tenure, fiscal autonomy, and Supreme Court administrative supervision.
- The hierarchy of courts matters even when the Supreme Court has concurrent jurisdiction.
- The power of the purse restricts Congress itself: no increases over the President's budget, no transfers except augmentation from savings, no spending without an appropriation.
- The Sandiganbayan is the "weird" collegiate court that works like a trial and appellate court for graft cases.
- Fact is what happened, law is how the law applies, and policy (political question) belongs to the executive, not the courts.
