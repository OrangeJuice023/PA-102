# Topic 3.6: The quest for the "Filipino"

> [!verify] \[NEEDS VERIFICATION\]

> [!plain] In plain words
> after Marcos concentrated power in one office, the 1987 Constitution added many safeguards. The ongoing debate is how to spread power better: [[Centralization vs Decentralization|decentralization]], [[Federalism|federalism]], or a different executive system (presidential, parliamentary, semi-presidential).

**Readings:** 1973 Constitution & 1978 Admin Code; 1986 Freedom Constitution; 1987 Constitution & Admin Code; **Abueva**, "Some Advantages of Federalism and Parliamentary Government for the Philippines" (2005); **Teehankee**, "Rationale and Features of Federalism" (2017).

**Big question:** how can Filipinos govern themselves **without reproducing excessive concentration of power**? The old colonial problem was a foreign power ruling Filipinos. Under Marcos, a **Filipino government** held concentrated power over Filipinos: **internal colonization**.

**General flow:** colonial centralization → centralized state → Marcos authoritarianism → concentration of power → EDSA 1986 → Freedom Constitution → constitutional redesign → centralization vs. decentralization → federalism → choice of executive system.

## Marcos regime and the 1973 Constitution

- The **1935 Constitution** allowed martial law for **invasion, insurrection, rebellion, or imminent danger thereof**.
- **1971 Constitutional Convention** was drafting a new constitution. Marcos used **presidential decrees** to push it through.
- **Ratification by Citizens' Assemblies** (barangay assemblies), **January 10–15, 1973**, by **show of hands / voice vote**. Sir's example: people were asked "Would you like free rice?" and photos of raised hands became "proof" of a yes vote. **Proclamation No. 1102** declared it ratified (effective about two days later).
- **1973 structure:** moved toward **parliamentary** government with **Transitory Provisions** and an **Interim National Assembly**.
  - **President = head of state; Prime Minister = head of government.**
  - **Unicameral Batasang Pambansa** (no Senate). Members = mambabatas pambansa; nicknamed "butasan pang-bulsa."
- **Presidential decrees:** the President made laws, so **executive + legislative power sat in one office**.
- **1976 amendment** let the President make laws whenever he wanted under martial law. **1976 and 1981 amendments** made the state highly centralized and authoritarian.
- Galindez: Marcos planned the authoritarian setup through studies in UP, involving two NCPAG professors.
- **Key issue:** a parliamentary transition on paper, but continued extraordinary presidential power in practice.

## After EDSA 1986

**Proclamation No. 3 (1986 Freedom Constitution):**

- A **transitional** constitution under **Corazon Aquino**, since there was no constitution after Marcos's exile.
- **Reorganized the government while a permanent constitution was being prepared**.
- **Removed** the Batasang Pambansa and the Prime Minister.
- **Temporarily gave the President legislative power**, since there was no working legislature.
- **Priorities:** government reorganization, human rights, recovering ill-gotten wealth, anti-corruption.

**Proclamation No. 9:** created an **appointive 50-member Constitutional Commission** (members from different areas and sectors) to draft the permanent constitution.

**1986 logic:** remove authoritarian structures → transitional executive framework → permanent democratic constitution.

## The 1987 Constitution

- **Presidential and unitary**, with republican government, [[Sovereignty|sovereignty]] of the people, [[Separation of Powers|separation of powers]], checks and balances, judicial independence, local [[Autonomy (CAR vs BARMM)|autonomy]], accountability of officials, **civilian supremacy**.
- Branded the **"charter of positive liberties"**: the only PH constitution to mention **love**; direct on **separation of church and state**; **empowers the judiciary** against the other branches (a shift **from legislative to judicial supremacy**).
- **Filipino ownership focus:** fear of foreign control made us **insular**.
- Martial law destroyed and centralized the industrial "movers," so we lost domestic capital; capital is still drained abroad (e.g., foreign-controlled telecoms).
- **Term limits** and **no presidential re-election**: a **double-edged sword**. Even a great president can't continue. Galindez's Mindanao example: defeating one dynasty often just starts a new one, since continuity seems the only way to sustain programs.
- **A reaction to martial law:** many fail-safes against corruption, yet corruption persists.
- **Sir's critique:** **too many fail-safes restrict us**. Example: martial law can't be declared even with China in our seas; only upon actual invasion (landfall), which may be too late.

## Centralization vs. decentralization

- **Logic of extraction:** Spanish, American, and Japanese centralized structures made taxation, resource extraction, policy enforcement, and political control easier. Model: **one nation → one central state**, despite huge regional and ethnolinguistic diversity.
- **Decentralization** = transfer of authority from the center to lower levels. PH steps:
  1. **Local Autonomy Act (1959):** early autonomy effort.
  2. **Decentralization Act (1967):** more admin authority transferred.
  3. **Local Government Code (1991, RA 7160):** major expansion of local powers.
- **De jure autonomy** (legal authority) **≠ practical/fiscal autonomy** (actual resources and capacity). An LGU can have legal powers but depend on the national government for revenue, funds, and capacity.

| Political decentralization | Economic decentralization |
| --- | --- |
| Transfer of **political authority**, admin functions, decisions | Transfer of **money**, revenue, economic capacity |

## Federalism

- Responds to excessive centralization: authority is **constitutionally divided** between national and regional/state governments.
- **Dual sovereignty:** both levels have constitutionally protected spheres.
- **Subsidiarity:** handle public matters at the **lowest capable level**.
- **Decentralization** = power passed **down**. **Federalism** = power **constitutionally divided**. **Unitary ≠ federal.**

| Model | System | Upper chamber | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **USA** | Presidential, territorial division | **Elected Senate** (state representation) | Separated executive and legislature |
| **Canada** | Parliamentary, federal/provincial spheres | **Appointed Senate** | Cultural and multinational accommodation |
| **Germany** | Parliamentary | **Bundesrat** (represents the **Länder** governments) | Strong regional administration |

**Memory:** USA = President + elected Senate; Canada = Parliament + appointed Senate; Germany = Parliament + Bundesrat.

## Executive systems

**Presidential:** President = head of state + head of government; President and legislature separately elected; fixed term.

- **Gridlock / dual legitimacy:** both President and legislature claim a popular mandate → conflict.
- **Personality and patronage politics:** celebrity, wealth, media, family names, weak parties, **turncoatism**, patronage, pork barrel.
- **Rigidity:** a fixed term makes changing the head of government hard outside impeachment or crisis.

**Parliamentary:** the Prime Minister heads the government and comes from the parliamentary majority. President/Monarch = head of state.

- **Fusion of powers:** legislature and executive are connected (electorate → assembly → cabinet headed by PM).
- Politics centers on **parties and programs**, not personalities and popularity. A politician rises through the party to become party leader.
- **Vote of no confidence:** parliament withdraws support → government replaced or new elections. A political **safety valve**.
- **Constructive vote of confidence (Germany):** parliament **removes and replaces** the PM at the same time. Memory: **REMOVE + REPLACE**.
- **Weakness: immobilism.** No majority or a fragmented party system → unstable government, **revolving PMs**. Examples: **France's Fourth Republic, Italy, post-Koizumi Japan.**

**Abueva's 8 advantages of parliamentary government:**

1. **Coordination:** fused executive and legislature.
2. **Prime Minister** emerges from parliament.
3. **Political parties** become democratic, disciplined, united, effective.
4. **Timely change of government** via no-confidence vote.
5. **Government program** shaped by the majority party.
6. **Party choice:** voters choose a party, not just candidates.
7. **Lower election cost:** no separate nationwide race for head of government.
8. **Less personality-based politics:** less emphasis on wealth, popularity, name recall.

**Semi-presidential (dual executive):** a **popularly elected President** (head of state, often defense and foreign affairs) + a **Prime Minister** accountable to the parliamentary majority (head of government, day-to-day). Same camp → easy coordination; opposing camps → conflict.

| System | Head of state | Head of government | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Presidential | President | President | Gridlock, personality politics, rigidity |
| Parliamentary | Monarch / President | **Prime Minister** | Immobilism, unstable government |
| Semi-presidential | Elected President | Prime Minister | President-PM conflict |

## Philippine federal proposals

| Proponent | Model | Structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Abueva / CMFP** | **Federal-parliamentary** | **11 autonomous states**, symbolic President (head of state), PM (head of government), bicameral parliament |
| **Pimentel Jr. / Senate Res. No. 10** | **Federal-presidential** | **12 autonomous regions**, presidential government, bicameral Congress, **equal Senate seats per region** |
| **Pimentel III / PDP-Laban** | **Federal-semi-presidential** | **11 regions**, French-style dual executive (President + PM), bicameral parliament |

**Memory:** ABUEVA = 11 + Parliamentary; PIMENTEL JR. = 12 + Presidential; PIMENTEL III = 11 + Semi-presidential.

- **Galindez's notes:** under **Gloria Arroyo**, a federal-parliamentary plan (11 states, President as figurehead, bicameral parliament) stalled because she wanted to continue as PM. Under Duterte, federalism was pushed more by his party than by Duterte himself.
- **Teehankee** discusses each system's pros and cons: parliamentary allows a no-confidence vote that can bring down a government; semi-presidential gives the executive more power but spreads it better.

## Constitutional interpretation: text and intent

1. **Text:** if the Constitution is **clear**, apply its **ordinary meaning**; no interpretation needed.
2. **Intent:** only if unclear, look at the records and the **inspiration** behind the provision (Sir mentioned 35 references for checking intent). If the source was "evil" (e.g., Marcos's martial law), later provisions were changed in reaction, such as the legal review of martial law.

**COMELEC example:** a rule required a majority vote of **all members** of the Commission; 3 of 7 didn't participate. The SC applied the **ordinary meaning**: count the **total composition**.

**Impeachment example (Art. XI, Sec. 3):**

- Conviction needs **two-thirds of all Members of the Senate**: **16 of 24** (Art. VI, Sec. 2 sets 24 senators). "Of the Senate" is clear text, so it can't be reinterpreted.
