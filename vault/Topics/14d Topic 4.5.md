# 4.5 The Government Corporate and Enterprise Bureaucracy

## Overview

The government corporate and enterprise [[Bureaucracy (Weber)|bureaucracy]] concerns the use of corporations and corporate-like entities to perform public functions, deliver services, or engage in economic activity. GOCCs can support economic development and allow the State to exercise proprietary functions through an organizational form different from an ordinary department or bureau.

The central issue is how to use the corporate form judiciously while ensuring that public resources and public purposes remain protected.

## A. GOCC Governance Act of 2011

**Republic Act No. 10149 — GOCC Governance Act of 2011**

The law establishes a [[Centralization|centralized]] framework for the governance and monitoring of GOCCs. The policy aims include:

- Using the corporate form only when it is appropriate and justified.
- Rationalizing and monitoring GOCC operations and resource allocation.
- Promoting transparent, responsible, and accountable governance.
- Establishing performance reporting and evaluation systems.
- Ensuring that governing boards are competent and act in the interest of the State.
- Adopting reasonable compensation and avoiding excessive remuneration.
- Separating regulatory functions from proprietary or commercial activities where required.

**Coverage and exclusions (Section 4).** The Act applies to GOCCs, GICPs/GCEs, and government financial institutions, including their subsidiaries — but excludes the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, state universities and colleges, cooperatives, local water districts, economic zone authorities, and research institutions. Proviso: in economic zone authorities and research institutions, the President appoints one-third of the board members from the list submitted by the GCG.

**Five statutory categories (Section 5(b)), distinct from the lecture's groupings below:**

1. Developmental/Social Corporations
2. Proprietary Commercial Corporations
3. Government Financial, Investment and Trust Institutions
4. Corporations with Regulatory Functions
5. Others as classified by the GCG

**Other mechanisms in the notes:**

- **Section 25:** every covered GOCC must maintain a website and publish the required information — audited financial and performance reports (latest annual report within 30 days of receipt), past five years of audited financial statements, quarterly/annual reports and trial balance, operating budget, compensation packages, borrowings, performance scorecards, subsidies and net lending, and government-guaranteed borrowings.
- **Section 26:** the 30 GOCCs with the highest total assets are subject to periodic special COA audits.
- **Section 27:** a government agency proposing to establish a GOCC or related corporation under the Corporation Code submits the proposal to GCG for review and recommendation to the President before SEC registration.
- **Section 28:** a government agency seeking to purchase a corporation or acquire controlling interest submits the proposal to GCG for review and presidential approval.

## B. Governance Commission for GOCCs (GCG)

RA 10149 created the **Governance Commission for GOCCs** as a central advisory, monitoring, and oversight body attached to the Office of the President.

**Composition.** Five members: the Chairperson (rank of Cabinet Secretary), two appointed members (rank of Undersecretary), and the Secretaries of DBM and DOF as ex officio members. The law initially provided ₱10 million from the President's Contingent Fund for operations, with subsequent funding included in the GAA.

**Authority.** The GCG formulates, implements, and coordinates GOCC governance policies; evaluates GOCC performance and relevance; can implement reorganization, merger, and streamlining, subject to the statutory framework and presidential direction; and recommends abolition or privatization to the President, with implementation following presidential approval. It establishes performance evaluation systems and scorecards, develops governance standards and ownership/operations manuals, evaluates board qualifications, and recommends compensation standards. It reports to the President and Congress, with the annual report due within 120 days **from the close of the year** (the notes say "120 days before the end of the year"; the Act states from the close of the year).

**The lecture's functional groupings.** The November 7 class notes group GOCC examples by function. This is the lecture's way of organizing examples, not a substitute for the five Section 5(b) statutory categories above:

- **Government Financial Institutions (GFIs):** LandBank, DBP, GSIS, SSS, and PDIC.
- **Public-service or utility entities:** PNOC, LRTA, tollway entities, PhilPost, PNCC (the notes add it may be developmental depending on the project), and MWSS. Some of these — notably LRTA and MWSS — are more precisely GICPs/GCEs than ordinary GOCCs under the Administrative Code; RA 10149 expressly includes GICPs/GCEs in its coverage, subject to Section 4.
- **Developmental or social-protection entities:** Pag-IBIG, GSIS, SSS, PhilHealth, and the Philippine Retirement Authority.
- **Proprietary or commercial entities:** PAGCOR and PCSO.

The **Games and Amusements Board** is kept separate as a regulatory agency/board with licensing and regulatory functions — it is not listed here as a proprietary/commercial GOCC.

These groupings do not all share the same strict legal classification: an example's actual charter, ownership, and statutory treatment must be checked before assigning it a formal category.

### UP and college foundations (lecture observation)

The Galindez notes state as shorthand that UP is a GOCC that does not answer to the GCG, and that college foundations are subsidiaries of UP and therefore GOCCs. This is preserved as the lecture's point about institutional exceptions — not as a settled legal rule. UP is a state university/college and a chartered institution under the Administrative Code framework; RA 10149 expressly excludes state universities and colleges from its coverage; and Supreme Court decisions have described UP as a government instrumentality rather than a GOCC in the strict Administrative Code sense.

> [!warning] Check
> The legal classification of a particular UP foundation or subsidiary cannot be inferred from its association with UP alone — its actual corporate form, ownership/control, charter, and relationship to the university must be checked. References: RA 10149, Sec. 4 (https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2011/ra_10149_2011.html); G.R. No. 237663 (https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2020/oct2020/gr_237663_2020.html).

## C. Important legal classifications under the Administrative Code

The notes cite the **Administrative Code of 1987, Introductory Provisions, Section 2(10)–(13)** (the notes write "Sec. 2(10–3)"). The definitions distinguish:

- **Instrumentality, Section 2(10):** an agency of the National Government not integrated within the departmental framework, vested by law with special functions or jurisdiction, endowed with some if not all corporate powers, administering special funds, and enjoying operational autonomy, usually through a charter. The definition includes regulatory agencies, chartered institutions, and GOCCs.
- **Regulatory agency, Section 2(11):** an agency expressly vested by law with jurisdiction to regulate, administer, or adjudicate matters affecting substantial rights and interests of private persons.
- **Chartered institution, Section 2(12):** an agency organized or operating under a special charter and vested with functions relating to specific constitutional policies or objectives. The definition includes state universities and colleges and the monetary authority.
- **GOCC, Section 2(13):** an agency organized as a stock or non-stock corporation, vested with functions relating to public needs whether governmental or proprietary in nature, and owned by the Government directly or through its instrumentalities either wholly or, in the case of stock corporations, to the extent of at least fifty-one percent (51%) of its capital stock.

**Key distinction:** A government instrumentality with corporate powers is not automatically a GOCC. Corporate powers alone do not determine the classification. The legal organization, charter, and statutory definition must be examined.

**For purposes of RA 10149**, Section 3(o) expands the term GOCC to include GICPs/GCEs and GFIs as defined in that Act. This does not erase the distinction under the general Administrative Code definition: an entity may be an instrumentality with corporate powers without being a GOCC in the strict Section 2(13) sense, while still falling within RA 10149's expanded coverage.

## D. Jurisprudence

### Alejandrino and Pasetes v. COA, G.R. No. 245400 (November 12, 2019)

**Facts and issue:** Officers of the Philippine National Construction Corporation (PNCC) were involved in the engagement of private lawyers. The payments of ₱911,580.96 were disallowed because the required written conformity and acquiescence of the Office of the Government Corporate Counsel and written concurrence of COA had not been secured, in violation of COA Circular No. 95-011 and OP-MC No. 9. The issues included PNCC's status as a GOCC, COA's audit authority, the validity of the disallowance, and the personal liability of the officers.

**Ruling and doctrine:**

- PNCC, 90.3% government-owned, was treated as a GOCC subject to COA audit jurisdiction despite having been incorporated under the general corporation law and lacking an original charter.
- Hiring private counsel by a GOCC is allowed only in exceptional circumstances and subject to the required approvals: the written conformity and acquiescence of the OGCC (or Solicitor General, as the case may be) and the written concurrence of COA.
- COA did not commit grave abuse of discretion in disallowing the payments.
- The lawyer-payees who had already rendered legal services in good faith were not required to refund the amounts they received. This payee-protection point is separate from the personal liability of the approving officers.
- The Court relieved Alejandrino and Pasetes of personal liability because their participation was ministerial — as Head of Human Resources and Administration and as Acting Treasurer — they were not involved in policy-making or decision-making on the hiring, and bad faith was not shown. This was the Court's case-specific ruling, not a blanket rule immunizing all officers involved in audit disallowances.

**Takeaway:** A COA disallowance does not automatically make every officer involved personally liable. The official's role in the transaction, actual participation in the decision, and good faith matter. For COA's side of the audit relationship, see [[14b Topic 4.3|Topic 4.3]] and [[14c Topic 4.4|Topic 4.4]].

### Light Rail Transit Authority v. City of Pasay, G.R. No. 211299 (June 28, 2022)

**Issue:** The City of Pasay assessed real property tax on properties associated with LRTA. LRTA argued that it was a government instrumentality with corporate powers rather than a GOCC and that its properties used for public service should be exempt.

**Ruling and doctrine:**

- LRTA was classified as a government instrumentality with corporate powers, not a GOCC in the strict Administrative Code sense.
- It has capital, but it is not divided into shares; it has no stockholders or voting shares. It is also not a non-stock corporation because it has no members.
- LRTA is attached to the DOTr, but attachment does not by itself mean it is integrated into the departmental framework.
- The LRTA properties **actually, solely, and exclusively devoted to public use**, consisting of the LRT railroads, terminals, and the lots on which they are situated, are exempt from real property tax. Not all LRTA properties are automatically exempt regardless of use.
- Portions leased to private parties are not exempt. The taxable private beneficial users, rather than LRTA, are liable for the corresponding real property tax.

**Takeaway:** Legal classification matters. Hindi porke may corporate powers ang isang government entity, GOCC na agad ito. Its legal form and the use of its property affect the applicable tax treatment.

**Date note:** The class notes list June 29, 2022, but the official decision is dated June 28, 2022.

## Key takeaways for 4.5

- GOCCs are tools for economic development and for exercising the State's proprietary functions.
- GCG provides centralized oversight across performance, disclosure, compensation, and rationalization.
- The five Section 5(b) statutory categories differ from the lecture's functional example groupings.
- GOCCs, instrumentalities with corporate powers, and chartered institutions are distinct classifications — and RA 10149's expanded coverage does not erase the difference.
- Always examine an entity's legal form — and, for property, its actual use.
- COA audit jurisdiction and personal liability are separate questions.
