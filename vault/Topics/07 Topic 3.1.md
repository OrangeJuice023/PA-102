# Topic 3.1: Precolonial period

> [!plain] In plain words
> there was no single Philippine nation-state, but there was still government. Communities were different from each other but connected by the sea, trade, marriage, and war. Barangays had leaders, laws, councils, and links across Asia.

**Big picture:** different peoples, languages, religions, environments → **connectivity** (trade, migration, marriage, warfare, sea routes) → **political organization** (barangays, chiefdoms, datus, alliances) → **no single Philippine nation-state**.

**Must-know distinctions:**

- **No nation-state ≠ no government.**
- **Political fragmentation ≠ political absence.**
- **Authority ≠ unlimited authority.**
- **Island ≠ isolated.**

## Burling: Hills and Plains

Southeast Asia was very diverse (geography, language, religion, culture, politics, ways of life) but shared regional patterns (monsoons, rivers, seas, mountains, plains).

|  | Hills | Plains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Terrain | Difficult | Easier communication |
| Population | Low density | Dense settlements |
| Farming | Shifting cultivation | Wet-rice agriculture |
| Religion | Indigenous/tribal religions more common | Major world religions more common |
| Politics | Many small communities, more local independence; harder to conquer | Larger, more integrated political communities |

The contrast is **not absolute**: they traded, migrated, fought, intermarried, and borrowed from each other. **Geography → way of life → population/communication → political organization.**

## Abinales & Amoroso: family and kinship

- **Cognatic kinship:** descent traced through **both** father's and mother's sides.
- **Fictive kinship:** non-blood relationships that create loyalty and cooperation networks.
- Politics ran on **kinship + loyalty + personal relationships**, not modern territorial citizenship.

> [!warning] Check
> Cognatic (bilateral) descent + fictive kinship (sanduguan, compadrazgo) = the relational fabric of precolonial politics, not territorial citizenship.

## "Baranganic" societies

Sir stresses that the barangay was **not just a small village**.

| Scholar | View |
| --- | --- |
| **Jocano** | The barangay had specialists, trade networks, social stratification, and a politico-administrative system, so for practical purposes it was a **State**. |
| **Junker** | More careful: a **pre-state complex society / chiefdom** (regionally integrated polities, hereditary elites, segmentary structures, temporary alliances). |

**A barangay as a political community had:** territorial rights, residence-based membership, government, customary law, punishment, and leadership. But it didn't work like today's fixed territorial state. Instead: **personal relationships → loyalty → overlapping influence → changing alliances** (the **mandala** idea).

## The datu and the system of government

- **Datu:** political and social leader. Functions: political and social leadership, legislation, judging, arbitration, mediation, warfare.
- **Other titles:** Rajah (larger barangays), Sultan (Islamic communities), Batara, Salipada, Sipad, Paduka.

| Role | Function |
| --- | --- |
| **Atubang sa Datu** | Administrative role under the datu |
| **Council of Elders** | Advisory and community authority |
| **Baylan / Catalonan** | **Spiritual authority**; could be a **woman or a queer man** (Sir) |
| **Umalohokan / Paratawag** | Announcer |
| **Tribute collector** | Collected tribute or contributions |

- **Point:** **functional specialization**. Authority was not concentrated in one person or one type of person.
- **Women:** female datus can't be proven or ruled out, but women clearly held spiritual authority as baylan/catalonan.
- **[[Sovereignty]] and alliances:** each barangay could be independent and see itself as the center, yet still form alliances.
- **Customary law and accountability:** even the datu could be held accountable. Examples: restitution for serious destruction, fines for theft, severe punishment for trespass, reciprocal sanctions for killings, gold fines for killing a free person. **Legitimacy came from customary law.**

## Junker: chiefdoms and sources

- Communities could have hereditary elites, political hierarchy, and economic specialization **without** becoming a [[Centralization|centralized]] state. **Hierarchy + fragmentation can coexist.**
- **Political economy:** foreign trade mattered but wasn't the whole economy. Also agriculture, local production, crafts, internal trade, prestige goods, tribute, warfare, social obligations. More political complexity came with more economic specialization.
- **Evidence:** Malay texts, Philippine oral traditions, Chinese tributary records, early Spanish writings, archaeology. **The sources don't always agree**: some show large, centralized, trade-linked systems; others show segmented communities.
- **Source criticism:** source → **author + context + purpose + worldview** → interpretation. Don't treat sources as automatically objective.

> [!warning] Check
> Junker's key distinction — chiefdom ≠ state (hereditary elites + regional integration + segmented structures can exist without centralization) — and the source-criticism framework (author+context+purpose+worldview) are lecture takeaways.

## Talib: The Historical Role of the Seas in the Malay World

- The sea was **not a barrier**. It was a **geo-political + geo-strategic + geo-economic** space that connected peoples, goods, and services.
- **Tanah air** ("land-water") / a **"water-land" or liquid world**: land and sea interconnected. **Sea → movement → trade → interaction → political/economic power.**
- **Malay World peoples:** Malays of the Straits of Malacca, Minangkabaus, Javanese, Bugis, Tagalogs, Tausugs.
- **Sir's major maritime societies:** **Tagalog, Tausug, Magindanawon, Marawi, Iranun/Illanun.** Major **non-maritime** society: **Ifugao**.

> [!warning] Check
> Talib's "tanah air" (land-water/liquid world) concept and the geo-political/strategic/economic sea space — the maritime societies list (Tagalog, Tausug, Magindanawon, Marawi, Iranun) vs. non-maritime (Ifugao) is Sir's specific classification.

## The Philippines in Maritime Asia (Abinales & Amoroso)

The PH was part of **Maritime Asia**, not an isolated archipelago: early polities, leadership, regional networks, social stratification, trade, tribute, warfare, ties within and beyond the islands.

| Year | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 618–906 | Contact with Tang-dynasty China begins |
| 900 CE | **Laguna Copperplate Inscription**: evidence of political and social hierarchies and ties |
| 982 | **Ma-i** (probably Mindoro) brings goods directly to Canton |
| 1001 | **Butuan** sends its first tribute mission to Song China |
| c. 1100 | Malays from Borneo settle in Manila/Tondo and intermarry with Tagalogs |
| c. 1275 | Arab missionaries and Chinese traders bring **Islam to Sulu** |
| 1277–1368 | Yuan-era trade grows with Butuan, Tanjay, Cebu |
| 1368–1424 | Sulu sends 6 missions to Ming China |
| c. 1450 | **Sayyid Abu Bakr** founds the **Sulu Sultanate** |
| 1521 | **Magellan** arrives |

> [!warning] Check
> The Maritime Asia timeline (Laguna Copperplate 900 CE, Butuan 1001, Islam to Sulu c.1275, Sulu Sultanate c.1450) — confirm the exact dates with Sir's slides; some sources vary by a few decades.

## Main idea of 3.1

The precolonial Philippines was **not a single nation-state**, but **not isolated primitive villages** either: **diverse communities + kinship + political organization + chiefdoms/barangays + customary law + accountability + maritime connectivity + trade, warfare, alliances = a complex precolonial political world.**
