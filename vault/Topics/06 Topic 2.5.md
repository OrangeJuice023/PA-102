# Topic 2.5: Public demands and the street-level dilemma

> [!plain] In plain words
> citizens want concrete things now (cheap food, jobs, less corruption), but government can only deliver within its laws, budget, and capacity. Frontline workers feel this squeeze most. Voters often choose by faction and region, not ideology. This is where Topics 2.3 and 2.5 come together.

**Readings:** Baroña / Pulse Asia (public demands); Esguerra (Robin Padilla video); **Dulay & Arguelles (2026)**, the main academic reading.

## 2.5.1 Public demands

- **Technical:** claims, expectations, needs, and preferences citizens direct toward government.
- **Layman:** "Ano ba gusto ng tao na gawin ng gobyerno?"
- **Examples:** lower prices, jobs, healthcare, education, security, infrastructure, accountability, welfare.
- **Public demand ≠ automatically a policy:** **demand → government → law + budget + capacity → policy → implementation.**
- Citizens ask: "Ano ang kailangan namin ngayon?" Government asks: "Ano ang kaya nating gawin legally, financially, and administratively?"

## 2.5.2 Baroña / Pulse Asia

- **Baroña (Manila Times, July 2026):** Filipinos want **lower food prices and more jobs**, meaning better living conditions and the ability to survive.
- **March 2026 Pulse Asia:** **affordable food 41%**, **reducing corruption 26%**, **jobs/livelihood 24%**.
- **July 2026 Pulse Asia:** face-to-face interviews with **2,400 Filipino adults**, nationwide, including 2028 electoral preferences.
- Pulse Asia describes its surveys as systematic measurement of public opinion on key issues.
- **Sir's point:** not the exact percentages, but that citizens have **concrete, immediate** demands.

## 2.5.3 Street-level dilemma

- **The dilemma:** general rules meet complicated individual lives.
- **Example:** a social worker has **100 applicants** but resources for **40**. Every applicant has a real need.
- **General rule** ("first come, first served") → **real people** (different needs and circumstances) → **limited resources** → **frontline decision**.
- **Key question:** how do public officials apply general rules to messy individual cases?

## 2.5.4 Formal vs. informal pressure at the frontline

| Formal pressure | Informal pressure |
| --- | --- |
| "Follow the rule." | "Ma'am, tulungan niyo naman ako. Kailangan talaga namin." / "Kakilala ko si Mayor." / "[[Utang na Loob\|Utang na loob]] naman." |

**[[Informal Institutions|Informal institutions]] don't stay outside government.** They enter the actual delivery of public services. That is PA 102 in one scenario.

## 2.5.5 Esguerra: Why Robin Padilla led the senatorial survey

- The point is not the prediction, but **why voters respond** to certain figures.
- Galindez's notes: surveys and lists can be biased; some have low response rates. Much of the vote is about **name, identity, and who they support**: Padilla backed Duterte, so a Duterte fanbase backs Padilla, regardless of credentials.
- Connects to Dulay & Arguelles: political behavior is not explained by platforms or ideology alone.

## 2.5.6 Dulay & Arguelles (2026): "The Contemporary Filipino Voter"

- **Question:** what divides voters when parties are weak and competition is personalistic?
- **Data:** nationally representative surveys before the **2025 senatorial elections**.
- **Weak party institutionalization:** PH politics is personalistic, factional, family-oriented, clientelist, and fluid.

|  | Party | Faction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Built on | Stable organization + program + political identity | Durable coalition around a leadership core, shared interests, repeated coordination |
| Question | "What does this organization stand for?" | "Whose camp are you in?" |

- **Factionalism** = patterned competition and bargaining between factions, often **across** formal party lines. It's not simply administration vs. opposition.

**4 voter groups:**

| Faction | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Duterte | 37.8% |
| Opposition | 26.4% |
| None | 21.2% |
| Marcos | 14.5% |

These figures describe that one dataset before the 2025 election, not all Filipino voters at all times.

**Main findings:**

- **Region is the strongest demographic divide:** Marcos → Rest of Luzon; Duterte → Mindanao; Opposition → Visayas. Voters also split by age, education, and urban-rural lines.
- **Ideology is weak:** most voters call themselves **centrists**; little left-right split.
- **Broad agreement** on redistributive economic policy. **Differences** on social issues: death penalty, mandatory ROTC, divorce.
- **Duterte alignment is the most electorally potent and polarizing:** Duterte supporters were much more likely to vote for Duterte-endorsed senatorial candidates.
- **Galindez's takeaway:** PH politics runs on factions, demographics, and loyalty, not even charisma or misinformation alone. Personality + faction + region + social identity, not just left vs. right.

## Why 2.5 matters for PA

The administrator works in **two worlds at once**:

- **The formal system:** rules, laws, budgets, procedures.
- **The social world:** relationships, expectations, identities, personal circumstances.

**Formal institutions ↔ informal institutions → actual behavior → governance outcome → public demands → street-level government ↔ real-world constraints.**
