## Foundational concepts (before Topic 2)

This part is in the exam coverage but sits before Topic 2. It comes from Galindez's lecture notes.

**In plain words:** a State needs people, territory, government, and sovereignty. The government acts for the State, and the bureaucracy does the day-to-day work. Public administration should be guided by values, not only by efficiency.

### Why we had the politics-administration dichotomy

- **Politics-administration dichotomy** = the old idea that politics (deciding) and administration (carrying out) should be kept separate.
- **Two causes:**
  1. The **patronage / spoils system** (jobs given as political rewards).
  2. The **Roaring 20s**: more wealth, and the belief that government shouldn't interfere with the private sector.
- Then the **Great Depression** hit. People realized that without government, no one saves the economy when it fails.
- **Dwight Waldo**: PA should follow a **pyramid of values**, not just the **3 Es** (efficiency, economy, effectiveness).
- **Sir's example of PA without values:** EO 66 under Duterte prioritized enforcing anti-drug laws. The dichotomy made PA "cold-hearted": it asked how to neutralize drug users, not why people turned to drugs or how health services could help.

### What is a State?

- **1987 Constitution:** the PH is a **democratic and republican state**; **sovereignty resides in the people** (Art. II, Sec. 1).

|  | Democratic state | Republican state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Direct exercise of democracy through representatives (conduit, agent, trustee) | **Survival**: strong military and strong economy |
| Risk | Mass power, bandwagoning, mob mentality | Concern with invaders and outsiders |
| Combined effect | Together they create the need to **qualify who is a citizen** (the in-group) |  |

- **4 elements of the State:** **People, Territory, Government, Sovereignty.** Memory trick: **PTGS**.
- **Poindexter v. Greenhow:** the State is an **"ideal person"**: intangible, so it can only act through a government.
- **Sovereignty** = free from external influence; can be **transferred to a successor**.

### What is a government?

**Government** = an **ensemble** of **political institutions** that impose a **mandate** and **rule** on **people** in a **territory**.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Ensemble** | The whole; parts working together as one entity |
| **Political institutions** | Have **agency** (capacity to act) and **normativity** (norms) |
| **Mandate** | To **make, enforce, and adjudicate** rules |
| **Adjudication** | **Inquisitorial** (judge takes part in investigating) or **adversarial** (neutral judge, two sides argue) |

### Types of government

- **De jure** = legitimate, **duly constituted**; sovereignty over the people.
- **De facto** = "of the fact": it occupies, so it rules (example: Japanese occupation). 3 kinds:
  1. **Legal sense:** possession of territory + control of the formal structure.
  2. **Paramount force:** an invading force overwhelms the defenders.
  3. **Revolt / insurrection:** an overwhelming force from inside the country.
- **Martial law** exists to counter paramount force and revolt/insurrection.

### The President's roles (from lecture)

Head of state, head of government, chief executive, general supervisor of LGUs, national service leader, commander-in-chief, policy leader. The President is a **trustee** representative. Other countries use a people's congress, a monarch, a president, or a prime minister.

### Bureaucracy (Max Weber)

- Bureaucracy = **rational-legal authority** + **division of labor (hierarchy)**.
- **6 features:** specialization (the "iron cage"), hierarchy, rational-legal rules, impersonality (dismissal only with proper cause), merit and fitness, security of tenure.
- **Why we have bureaucracy (6 reasons):** public goods, externalities, controlling monopolies, redistribution of wealth, social regulation, information asymmetry.
- **Policy capacity** = can the state achieve its goals? **State capacity** = does the state have the resources?

| Polity | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Bureaucratic polity** | Run by a **technocratic elite** |
| **Proprietary polity** | **Weak state institutions**, strong **resource elites**. Sir's point: in the PH, resource elites dictate what state institutions do. |

> **Check:** The "iron cage" reference is Weber's metaphor for the dehumanizing effects of rationalization; it is not a separate feature but describes the consequence of specialization + impersonality.

## Topic 2: Institutional overview of the Philippines

**In plain words:** to understand a government, compare what is written, what people believe, what actually exists, and what is actually needed. Reform should come from Filipino values, because borrowed rules get bent by local practice.

### 4 ways to understand an organization or government

| Way | What you look at | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Written down** | Constitution, statutes (Congress), admin issuances (President, admin agencies), jurisprudence or **"case law"** (Supreme Court) | The formal, legal picture |
| 2. **Believed to exist** | People's own perceptions of government | Often goes **against** what is written |
| 3. **Actually exists** | Org charts, citizen's charters, mission and vision, each office's real hierarchy | Hard to see: you need the issue first, and to find it you must **diagnose** (organizational diagnosis) |
| 4. **Actually needed** | The government we need after finding and diagnosing the issues | From Galindez's lecture notes |

Different scholars give different perspectives on what is needed.

### Casis: "If I Were to Reform Philippine Society"

- **Main point:** to fix PH governance, base reform on **Filipino-owned values**.
- We had a **fused society**: all powers (executive, judicial, legislative, warrior, economist, religious leader, administrator) sat in one person:
  - **Lakan** in the north
  - **Datu** in the middle
  - **Sultan** in the south
- We then **borrowed separation of powers** from the West. We need our **own** values.
- PH governance misses the **means of doing** (the "why" and "how").
- Links to **Waldo's pyramid/pinnacle of values**: all that we do is based on collective values.
- After Marcos we added safeguards: **judicial review** (no more abuse of presidential decrees) and the **Ombudsman**.
- Why we needed separation of powers in the Filipino context: values like **utang na loob** and **bayanihan** produced favoritism and corruption.
- **Conclusion:** values make up the reforms we have.

> **Check:** Casis's "fused society" concept maps directly to the barangay datu holding multiple roles (executive, judicial, legislative, religious) — the precolonial baseline before Western separation of powers was borrowed.

### Tiglao: "The Decapitalization of the Philippines"

- **Filipino First policy**, started under **Pres. Carlos P. Garcia**: local businesses and products get priority over foreign ones. Now **Art. XII, Sec. 10** of the 1987 Constitution. Today we call it **economic nationalism**.
- **Filipino requirement:** (1) a majority of the board is Filipino, and (2) **60%** of the company is Filipino-owned.
- **Problem:** core telecom companies that should be Filipino-owned are really **foreign-controlled**. Profits go out (traded in Hong Kong) instead of funding local infrastructure.
- **How:** **"voting preferred shares"** dilute foreign ownership **on paper**, so the company appears Filipino-owned.
- **Lesson:** formal institutions can be bypassed by informal procedures, while formal laws are used to make it look legitimate.

> **Check:** Tiglao's "decapitalization" thesis — foreign control of strategic industries despite Filipino ownership requirements — illustrates how formal rules (Art. XII, Sec. 10) can be structurally evaded through financial engineering (voting preferred shares).

### Constitutional supremacy and hierarchy of laws

**Readings:** Civil Code Art. 7; Manila Prince Hotel; Tañada; Sameer Overseas.

- The Constitution is the **supreme law** that sets the boundaries of public power. Hierarchy: **Constitution → statutes → administrative rules/orders.**
- **4 keywords (Sir's):**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Supreme** | Highest law of the land |
| **Imperious** | Must be taken seriously |
| **Absolute** | Applies to all branches |
| **Unalterable** | No ordinary law can go against it |

## Topic 2.3: Informal institutions

**In plain words:** informal institutions are unwritten social rules that still shape behavior. Filipino values like utang na loob are not good or bad by themselves. The problem starts when they override fairness and accountability.

- **Formal institution** = what the rules officially say.
- **Informal institution** = socially established rules, norms, relationships, or practices that shape behavior even when not written into law.

| Value | Good side | Problem side | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Utang na loob** | Gratitude, reciprocity, communal solidarity | Favoritism, patronage, vote-buying | Cleofas; De Joya et al. |
| **Diskarte** | Creative problem-solving under constraints | Used to excuse fixers, rule-bending, kickbacks | Morales; Hilotin |
| **Pakikisama** | Cooperation, harmony, social cohesion | Conformity, covering up a patron's misdeeds | Ibe |
| **Hiya** | Consideration for others | Silence; "wag na, nakakahiya" stops reporting | Mendoza; De Joya |

### 2.3.1 Utang na loob (Cleofas)

- A **"debt of good will"** from receiving someone's kindness. "Tinulungan niya ako, kaya may obligasyon akong suklian."
- **Not automatically bad.** Cleofas asks how to tell its good forms from the forms that lead to nepotism, patronage, corruption, and injustice.
- **Good form:** gratitude, reciprocity, helping those who helped you, keeping relationships, communal solidarity.
- **Problem form:** when personal obligation conflicts with fairness or others' interests: personal favor → obligation → "may utang ako sa kanya" → favoritism → others disadvantaged.
- **Key point:** utang na loob must go **with katarungan (justice)**, especially fair distribution of resources and opportunities, so it leads to **pagkakaisa** and communal flourishing.
- **Pedro and Juan:** Juan paid Pedro's tuition, then lost his job. Pedro pulls strings to get Juan a job in his company. That stretches utang na loob too far. The right way: help Juan find the best job **for himself**, not play savior or one-up him.

**Patron-client (parientes-padrinos):** patron gives resources, access, help → client feels gratitude, obligation, loyalty → back to the patron. Help often comes during **kasal, binyag, libing**, and fiestas.

**Why it matters for PA:** weak public service → personal connection → special access → utang na loob. Government services start to feel like personal favors. **Personal relationships can make up for weak formal institutions, but they can also keep them weak.**

### 2.3.2 De Joya et al. (2024): Utang na loob and voting

- **Method:** 8 registered voters in **Bacoor and Imus, Cavite**; semi-structured interviews; Jocano's **Smooth Interpersonal Relations (SIR)** model + a **consequentialist** framework.
- **SIR:** the importance of keeping harmonious relationships (harmony, belongingness, reciprocity, hiya, avoiding conflict).
- **What happens:** politicians give money, food, liquor, or gifts, even months or years before elections, especially at **kasal, binyag, libing** (a kind of social bribery). "Special feeling kapag napuntahan ka ni mayor." The **kumpadre system** speeds up utang na loob.
- **Chain:** favor → gratitude → obligation → loyalty.
- **Findings:**
  - Lower-income voters are targeted more; the guilt of debt is stronger the lower the class.
  - Most respondents said utang na loob is **not** an ethical basis for voting, since the services are part of the politician's job, **except Class E**, who valued immediate help.
  - People still vote for these politicians because of their existing power, lack of opposition, and immediate benefits.
- **The problem isn't gratitude itself.** It's when **personal obligation overrides fair evaluation** of candidates and performance.
- **Limit:** a small qualitative study, so it **can't be generalized** to all Filipino voters.

### 2.3.3 Diskarte

**Morales (2017):** diskarte = **creative problem-solving in response to practical problems and situational constraints**. "Anong paraan ang pwede kong gawin given my actual situation?"

- **Connected to:** divergent thinking, flexible thinking, making remote associations, creativity, resourcefulness, adapting to constraints.
- **Personality traits that help:** openness to experience, "psychopathic boldness," and accepting some (but not all) social norms.
- **Key element:** **constraints** from one's environment, especially inequality in social position or resources.
- **Formula:** situational constraint → creative/flexible thinking → alternative solution → desired outcome.
- **Diskarte ≠ simply being intelligent. Diskarte ≠ exactly creativity:** diskarte is **reactive and adaptive**, not just proactive.
- **Practical ≠ automatically ethical.** A solution can be clever and effective but still unfair, harmful, or illegal. Morales flags ethics because diskarte affects other people.

**Hilotin (2026), Gulf News:**

- Diskarte can mean "hustle + rule-bending + shakedown artistry": the language of diskarte makes **fixers, rule-bending, kickbacks** sound like clever survival.
- Diskarte is "hard-coded": Filipino upbringing admires a ma-diskarte family member, for good or bad, as long as it benefits the family or tribe.
- Our **culture of impunity** clashes with our dream to be the next **Singapore**: corruption becomes **high-gain, low-risk**.
- **Tribalism** (loyalty over facts):
  - Selective blindness: "Magnanakaw siya, pero amin siya."
  - Conspiracy framing: evidence dismissed as "paninira."
  - Regionalism: loyalty, not competence, decides governance.
  - Moral outsourcing: leaders forgiven if they "deliver."
  - Whataboutism: "Bakit ako lang? E lahat naman kami?"
- **Solutions:** (1) transparency, (2) blockchain-tagged spending, (3) real consequences and punishment, (4) cut off the source: dynasties, fixer politics; reward whistleblowers.

**Diskarte as adaptive problem-solving vs. diskarte used to justify breaking rules.**

> **Check:** Morales's definition (reactive, adaptive, constraint-driven) and Hilotin's critique (diskarte as excuse for rule-bending/fixer culture) represent the two lecture frames — confirm both were covered.

### 2.3.4 Pakikisama (Ibe)

- **Pakikisama** = getting along; maintaining harmonious relationships.
- **Positive:** cooperation, social cohesion, community. **Negative:** conformity, avoiding needed disagreement, protecting relationships over accountability.
- **Ibe:** to be "mabuting makisama" after receiving a patron's favor means acting with tact and dignity, but also **turning a blind eye** to the patron's misdeeds.
- **"Extended political family":** family-oriented ties extend into politics, feeding patronage and **political dynasties**. Example in the reading: Duterte supporters brushing aside his misdeeds and urging votes for his family members.
- **Pakikisama is passed down:** family shapes political views, so loyalties carry across generations and create dynasties.
- **Pakikisama itself is not the problem.** The issue is when harmony means ignoring misconduct or putting loyalty above accountability.

> **Check:** Ibe's concept of "extended political family" links directly to how personal/family loyalty feeds patronage and dynasties — the lecture connects this to Duterte-era dynamics.

### 2.3.5 Hiya

- **Hiya** = shame; sensitivity to social judgment; concern about embarrassment and relationships.
- **Positive:** "Ayokong mapahiya yung ibang tao" → consideration. **Negative:** "Ayokong magsalita kasi baka ma-offend sila" → silence.
- **PA example:** formal rule: "Report the wrongdoing." Informal pressure: "Wag na, nakakahiya." Result: **silence**.
- **Mendoza (2025):** hiya, tino, and bait are not fixed traits but **"vernacular grammars"** people use to judge political scandals.
- **Galindez:** hiya can be **abused** for social control ("walang hiya," "ano ba sasabihin ng iba?"). "Walang hiya" is a **colonial** idea not grounded in **kapwa**, tied to **panghihimasok** (entering without consent) and **pananakop** (taking without consent).
- **Hiya + pakikisama:** when the ICC arrested Duterte, supporters claimed disgracing him disgraced the whole country.

> **Check:** Mendoza's "vernacular grammars" (hiya, tino, bait as interpretive lenses for political scandals) and Galindez's colonial critique of "walang hiya" (linking it to panghihimasok/pananakop, not kapwa) — both are from the lecture readings.

## Topic 2.4: Context of Philippine governance

**In plain words:** you can't understand Philippine governance by looking only at formal structures. Filipino social life existed long before the modern state, and history, identity, culture, regional diversity, and values all shape how government works today.

**Why this topic matters:** Filipino social organization (kinship, alliances, rituals, community, social obligations) existed **before** the modern state. Institutions didn't suddenly appear in 1987.

**Readings in order:** (1) Ayala Museum, (2) Niels Mulder, (3) Raul de Guzman, (4) Filipino Story Studio, (5) FEATR, (6) J.W. Diokno's letter.

### 2.4.1 Ayala Museum: "How Different were Early Filipino Traditions?"

- Life is marked by **3 stages: birth (life), marriage or alliance, death.** Human experience is both **universal and cultural**.
- Early Filipinos slept on palm-leaf mats; houses were on **stilts** (protection from drafts, dirt, pests), north and south.
- Skilled **weavers** (abundant fibers) and **metalworkers** (brass, bronze, gold).
- **Weddings** were community affairs; the man took the woman's name; **divorce** existed, with property split by who was at fault.
- Regardless of belief, they believed in an **afterlife**.

> **Check:** The Ayala Museum notes emphasize material culture (stilts, weaving, metalworking) and social practices (community weddings, divorce, matrilocal naming) as evidence of precolonial sophistication — confirm the marriage/property details match Sir's lecture.

### 2.4.2 Niels Mulder: "Filipino Identity: The Haunting Question"

- **Big question:** what does it mean to be Filipino, and why does PH governance look the way it does?
- He links identity to Spanish colonial history, American imperialism, cultural colonization, Southeast Asian social organization, and nationalism.
- **Central concern:** the **gap between the state (public sphere) and people's everyday world**. Systemic divides make it hard for Filipinos to identify with the collective whole and become a nation of committed citizens.
- **Family-centered** life (bilateral kinship, debts of gratitude) competes with loyalty to the larger political community.
- **Chain:** history → culture/identity → social relations → political behavior → governance.

> **Check:** Mulder's core argument — the state/everyday world gap and family-centered life competing with national identification — is the lecture's key lens for why governance struggles.

### 2.4.3 Raul P. de Guzman: "Is There a Philippine Public Administration?" (1986)

- **Answer: yes**, as much as there is a French, American, or Japanese PA. But the question must be defined properly.
- **"Public administration" has 3 meanings:**
  1. Government in action / the bureaucracy
  2. The field of study
  3. The profession of managing government affairs
- **The real PA 102 question:** what makes PH PA **"Philippine"**? De Guzman asks whether our bureaucracy has **structural** and **behavioral** characteristics that are uniquely Filipino, versus traits shared with bureaucracies elsewhere.

## Topic 3.1: Precolonial period

**In plain words:** there was no single Philippine nation-state, but there was still government. Communities were different from each other but connected by the sea, trade, marriage, and war. Barangays had leaders, laws, councils, and links across Asia.

**Big picture:** different peoples, languages, religions, environments → **connectivity** (trade, migration, marriage, warfare, sea routes) → **political organization** (barangays, chiefdoms, datus, alliances) → **no single Philippine nation-state**.

**Must-know distinctions:**

- **No nation-state ≠ no government.**
- **Political fragmentation ≠ political absence.**
- **Authority ≠ unlimited authority.**
- **Island ≠ isolated.**

### Burling: Hills and Plains

Southeast Asia was very diverse (geography, language, religion, culture, politics, ways of life) but shared regional patterns (monsoons, rivers, seas, mountains, plains).

|  | Hills | Plains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Terrain | Difficult | Easier communication |
| Population | Low density | Dense settlements |
| Farming | Shifting cultivation | Wet-rice agriculture |
| Religion | Indigenous/tribal religions more common | Major world religions more common |
| Politics | Many small communities, more local independence; harder to conquer | Larger, more integrated political communities |

The contrast is **not absolute**: they traded, migrated, fought, intermarried, and borrowed from each other. **Geography → way of life → population/communication → political organization.**

### Abinales & Amoroso: family and kinship

- **Cognatic kinship:** descent traced through **both** father's and mother's sides.
- **Fictive kinship:** non-blood relationships that create loyalty and cooperation networks.
- Politics ran on **kinship + loyalty + personal relationships**, not modern territorial citizenship.

> **Check:** Cognatic (bilateral) descent + fictive kinship (sanduguan, compadrazgo) = the relational fabric of precolonial politics, not territorial citizenship.

### "Baranganic" societies

Sir stresses that the barangay was **not just a small village**.

| Scholar | View |
| --- | --- |
| **Jocano** | The barangay had specialists, trade networks, social stratification, and a politico-administrative system, so for practical purposes it was a **State**. |
| **Junker** | More careful: a **pre-state complex society / chiefdom** (regionally integrated polities, hereditary elites, segmentary structures, temporary alliances). |

**A barangay as a political community had:** territorial rights, residence-based membership, government, customary law, punishment, and leadership. But it didn't work like today's fixed territorial state. Instead: **personal relationships → loyalty → overlapping influence → changing alliances** (the **mandala** idea).

### The datu and the system of government

- **Datu:** political and social leader. Functions: political and social leadership, legislation, judging, arbitration, mediation, warfare.
- **Other titles:** Rajah (larger barangays), Sultan (Islamic communities), Batara, Salipada, Sipad, Paduka.

| Role | Function |
| --- | --- |
| **Atubang sa Datu** | Administrative role under the datu |
| **Council of Elders** | Advisory and community authority |
| **Baylan / Catalonan** | **Spiritual authority**; could be a **woman or a queer man** (Sir) |
| **Umalohokan / Paratawag** | Announcer |
| **Tribute collector** | Collected tribute or contributions |

- **Point:** **functional specialization**. Authority was not concentrated in one person or one type of person.
- **Women:** female datus can't be proven or ruled out, but women clearly held spiritual authority as baylan/catalonan.
- **Sovereignty and alliances:** each barangay could be independent and see itself as the center, yet still form alliances.
- **Customary law and accountability:** even the datu could be held accountable. Examples: restitution for serious destruction, fines for theft, severe punishment for trespass, reciprocal sanctions for killings, gold fines for killing a free person. **Legitimacy came from customary law.**

### Junker: chiefdoms and sources

- Communities could have hereditary elites, political hierarchy, and economic specialization **without** becoming a centralized state. **Hierarchy + fragmentation can coexist.**
- **Political economy:** foreign trade mattered but wasn't the whole economy. Also agriculture, local production, crafts, internal trade, prestige goods, tribute, warfare, social obligations. More political complexity came with more economic specialization.
- **Evidence:** Malay texts, Philippine oral traditions, Chinese tributary records, early Spanish writings, archaeology. **The sources don't always agree**: some show large, centralized, trade-linked systems; others show segmented communities.
- **Source criticism:** source → **author + context + purpose + worldview** → interpretation. Don't treat sources as automatically objective.

> **Check:** Junker's key distinction — chiefdom ≠ state (hereditary elites + regional integration + segmented structures can exist without centralization) — and the source-criticism framework (author+context+purpose+worldview) are lecture takeaways.

### Talib: The Historical Role of the Seas in the Malay World

- The sea was **not a barrier**. It was a **geo-political + geo-strategic + geo-economic** space that connected peoples, goods, and services.
- **Tanah air** ("land-water") / a **"water-land" or liquid world**: land and sea interconnected. **Sea → movement → trade → interaction → political/economic power.**
- **Malay World peoples:** Malays of the Straits of Malacca, Minangkabaus, Javanese, Bugis, Tagalogs, Tausugs.
- **Sir's major maritime societies:** **Tagalog, Tausug, Magindanawon, Marawi, Iranun/Illanun.** Major **non-maritime** society: **Ifugao**.

> **Check:** Talib's "tanah air" (land-water/liquid world) concept and the geo-political/strategic/economic sea space — the maritime societies list (Tagalog, Tausug, Magindanawon, Marawi, Iranun) vs. non-maritime (Ifugao) is Sir's specific classification.

## Topic 3.2: Spanish colonization (1521; 1565 to 1898)

**In plain words:** Spain built a centralized colonial state that mixed church, military, and government power. Filipinos first asked for reforms inside Spain, then fought for independence, and built their own government along the way. The Revolution was also state-building.

### Conquest and the colonial state

- **Sir's conquest structure:** **Friar → Captain → Army** (religious + political + military power).
- **1521** = first contact (**Magellan**). **1565** = permanent rule begins (**Legazpi**).
- **Precolonial political communities → Spanish conquest → colonial state.**
- **Elizalde:** Spanish rule (1565–1898) changed over time: different colonial models and economic regimes reorganized political, economic, social, and cultural life.

**Colonial hierarchy:** King of Spain → Consejo de Indias / Ministerio de Ultramar → Viceroyalty of Nueva España (until 1821) → Governor-General → Provinces → Pueblos/Municipalities → Barrios.

| Level | Office | Functions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central | **Governor-General** (Gobernador y Capitán General) | Representative of the King, commander-in-chief, chief justice, vice-royal patron. One office, many powers. |
| Provincial | **Alcaldía**: **Alcalde Mayor** | Broad executive, judicial, police, tribute, inspection, patronage, military functions. Could trade under the **indulto de comercio** (Agoncillo). |
| Provincial | **Corregimiento**: **Corregidor** |  |
| Pueblo | **Gobernadorcillo** | Head of the municipality. Filipinos and Chinese mestizos could serve; needed age, Spanish literacy, and prior service as cabeza de barangay. Aides: directorcillo, alguaciles, tenientes de justicia. |
| Barrio | **Cabeza de Barangay** | Tax/tribute collection, peace and order, recruiting **polistas**, local admin. Tenientes del barrio. |

### Accountability: Residencia and Visita

|  | Residencia | Visita |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When | **After** the official's term | **During** the term |
| By whom | Investigation of the official's conduct | A **Visitador-General** |

Possible penalties: fines, dismissal, imprisonment, property sequestration.

> **Check:** Residencia (post-term) vs. Visita (during term, by Visitador-General) — both could impose fines, dismissal, imprisonment, property sequestration.

### Church, economy, and society

- **Church and State** were deeply intertwined. The clergy influenced civil administration, education, censorship, land, economic affairs, local politics. **Friarocracy / monastic supremacy** became a major 19th-century criticism → **separation of Church and State** in Malolos.
- **Economic institutions:** taxation/tribute; **polo y servicio personal** (forced community labor); **encomienda** (grants of tribute rights over populations); **Galleon Trade** (Manila–Acapulco); Real Sociedad Económica de Amigos del País; Royal Philippine Company.
- **República de Españoles vs. República de Indios:** separate legal orders under the Recopilación de Leyes de los Reinos de las Indias.
- Spanish rule reorganized society through admin categories, taxes, labor duties, religion, education, economic rules, changing local elites.
- **Habsburg → Bourbon:** more **centralist, interventionist, reform-oriented**.

> **Check:** The Habsburg→Bourbon shift (more centralist, interventionist, reform-oriented) and the Friarocracy→Malolos Church-State separation link are lecture framing points.

### Cádiz Constitution of 1812

- **Core ideas:** national sovereignty, constitutional monarchy, separation of powers, representation, individual liberties, political participation.
- The PH was recognized as part of the Spanish nation with **representation in the Cortes**. Initial goal: **reform + representation + assimilation**.
- Later: stronger Spanish intervention in local government and **removal of PH representation**.
- **Aguilar (2025)** studies this tension.
- **High-yield line:** **constitutional equality on paper ↔ colonial hierarchy in practice.**

> **Check:** Cádiz 1812 gave PH representation in the Cortes (constitutional equality on paper) while colonial hierarchy persisted in practice — Aguilar (2025) studies this tension.

### From reform to revolution

**La Propaganda:** sought change **within** the Spanish system: equality of Filipinos and Spaniards, PH representation, restored Filipino role in local government, reforms in Church and government.

|  | La Liga Filipina | Katipunan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founded | **1892**, by **José Rizal** | **1892** |
| Goal | **Reform**: unity, mutual protection, defense against injustice, education, agriculture, commerce, social reform | **Independence** + revolutionary action |
| Nature | Reformist, **not armed** | **Political and moral**, not only military |
| Structure | **Supreme Council → Provincial Councils → Popular Councils** | Central council + local units |
| Fate | Weakened after Rizal's arrest and deportation | Led the Revolution |

**Kartilya ng Katipunan:** equality, brotherhood, defense of the oppressed, good character, duty, respect for women, equality regardless of status or race. **Formula: personal morality → social equality → national liberation.**

**Rizal and the Noli (Schumacher):** the Noli was a **catalyst**, not the direct cause, of the Revolution. Don't reduce it to "Noli → Revolution." It helped people understand colonial society, build national consciousness, question inequality, and think about Filipino identity. **Rizal's path: reform → disillusionment → national consciousness → eventual independence.**

> **Check:** La Liga (reformist, Rizal, 1892) vs. Katipunan (independence, revolutionary, 1892) — the Kartilya formula (personal morality → social equality → national liberation) and Schumacher's catalyst thesis (Noli ≠ direct cause) are lecture frames.

### Revolutionary governments

- **Tejeros Convention (March 22, 1897):** **Magdiwang vs. Magdalo** (Cavite factions). Question: should the Katipunan stay the revolutionary government, or be replaced? A central revolutionary government was set up: **Aguinaldo President**, **Mariano Trías VP**. The **Bonifacio-Tirona** conflict created a **legitimacy crisis**. Katipunan as authority → formal revolutionary government → legitimacy problem.
- **Departmental Government of Central Luzon:** President, VP, Treasury, Interior, War, Welfare. Revolutionaries were already experimenting with **departmental government**, not just military command.
- **Biak-na-Bato Republic:** President, VP, and departments (Treasury, Interior, War, Foreign Affairs). Its provisional constitution recognized religious liberty, education, press freedom, freedom of professions. Moved toward an independent state while keeping some reformist demands.
- **Hong Kong Junta:** center for diplomacy, financing, planning, procurement, political decisions during exile.
- **Aguinaldo's governments:** **Pangulo → Dictator → Revolutionary Government → Constitutional Republic.**

> **Check:** The revolutionary government sequence (Tejeros → Departmental Govt → Biak-na-Bato → Hong Kong Junta → Aguinaldo's sequence: Pangulo→Dictator→Revolutionary Govt→Constitutional Republic) — the Bonifacio-Tirona legitimacy crisis at Tejeros is the key fracture point.

### Malolos Republic

- Brought together executive departments, legislature, local government, judiciary, and constitutional rules.
- **Malolos Constitution:** free and independent republic, popular sovereignty, representative and responsible government, 3 branches, religious freedom, **separation of Church and State**.
- **Structure:** Republic → Legislature (Assembly), Executive (President + Cabinet), Judiciary (Supreme Court). Features: separation of powers, continuing legislature, strong President of the Cabinet, accountability to the legislature, indirect elections.
- **Sir's final slide:** a shift toward **Haring Bayang Katagalugan**, later **Filipinas**; separation of Church and State; national unity; international recognition; government should **not stay in military hands**.

> **Check:** Malolos Constitution (popular sovereignty, 3 branches, Church-State separation) — the tension: on paper it looked parliamentary (President elected by Assembly), but Art. 99 transitory provisions gave Aguinaldo decree powers during the war. Agpalo calls it a "true pangulo regime" — this tension is revisited in Topic 4.1.

| La Liga / Propaganda | Katipunan and after |
| --- | --- |
| Representation + equality + reform | Independence + self-determination + national unity |

Resistance **before** La Liga was often local, economic, parochial, and defensive of native ways.

## Topic 3.3: American colonization (1898 to 1946) \[NEEDS VERIFICATION\]

**\[NEEDS VERIFICATION\] Topics 3.3 to 4.1 come from your uncertain notes, cross-checked with Galindez's lecture notes. Double-check them with your classmates before you memorize.** Lines marked **Check:** are where the notes disagree with each other or with standard history.

**In plain words:** the US built the PH state through a civil service, English-language schools, and a military police, and slowly let Filipinos take part. But the US kept sovereignty. This is where our militarized governance and copied American system come from.

### Legal status (Galindez)

- The PH was an **unincorporated territory** of the US.
- The **First Philippine Republic never had sovereignty**; it was a **belligerent de facto** government. Sovereignty passed from **Spain to the US**, so the US represented the PH internationally and replaced the de facto Philippine government.
- The Americans removed the powers Filipinos had over local governments and replaced them.

### McKinley's orders (Galindez)

1. Establish order and municipal government.
2. Build the state and conquer the rest of the territory (Mindanao).
3. Govern for the happiness of Filipinos, **even conforming to their prejudices** (Christian vs. non-Christian), so government could discriminate against non-Christians.

### State-building: McKinley's 3 priorities (Abinales)

1. **Bureau of Civil Service:** the **first bureau** created. "Greatest care should be taken in the selection of officials." Heads were American civilians or honorably discharged soldiers, "men of the highest character and fitness," free from partisan politics. Two goals: (a) be different from Spanish rule; (b) politics must not undermine the bureaucracy. The civil service was **"a regime of law, not of men."** First run by military men and American civilians; few Filipinos in executive posts.
2. **Public education:** free elementary and secondary schooling, because a democracy needs literate citizens. **English** as the common language, since Filipinos had no unified language. The first English-speaking Filipinos became teachers. Spain had avoided teaching Spanish widely for fear it would give Filipinos too much **cohesion** and lead to uprisings. Tagalog was considered (newspapers used it), but the Americans **vetoed vernaculars** until the end of colonial rule.
3. **Philippine Constabulary (1901 to 1991):** a **gendarmerie** (military doing law enforcement) replacing the insular police. Regulated firearms, handled **health emergencies** (no health department yet), expanded telegraph and postal systems, suppressed brigandage and insurrection, supervised local police.

- **Sir's point:** the Constabulary is why PH governance is **militarized**: military and police handle disasters and health crises, even the pandemic. The US wanted to demilitarize colonies but keep a security force. It was raised to **suppress its own people**, which shaped soldiers' attitudes during martial law.
- **Failures:** **land policy** (land reform without support; farmers went back to tenancy) and **tax policy** (one peso for a poor peasant, only 35 pesos for a rich landowner).

> **Check:** **Civil Service Act (Act No. 5) was enacted September 19, 1900** — do not memorize "November 31, 1900" (November has 30 days).

> **Check:** **Rafael Crame** became the **first Filipino Chief of the Philippine Constabulary in 1917**. He was not the first chief overall.

> **Check:** The **Commonwealth was inaugurated November 15, 1935** — the 1936 date in some notes refers to Constitution Day, not the Commonwealth inauguration.

### Special provinces (Mindanao, Cordillera)

- Areas Spain barely colonized, mostly non-Christian. Both were held under **military control**; some local leaders saw the US as protection for their trade from Filipino control.
- **Two governments (Galindez):** the main Government of the PH and the special **Moro Province** under a military governor. The Americans had no prior experience with Islam. The Moro Province was never integrated; local leaders stayed under American control.
- **Bureau of Non-Christian Tribes:** shifted from religious to **ethno-racial** categories; said **"Filipino" applied only to Christians**. Non-Christians were still seen as uncivilized.
- Constabulary officers thought integration was **two generations away**. The army eventually handed power to civilian leaders in Manila.
- **Legacy:** non-Christians became "minority outsiders," and the **American-drawn map** shaped the Bangsamoro territory Islamic movements fight for.
- The **Sultan of Sulu** surrendered sovereignty through a **memorandum agreement** with the US (Galindez).

> **Check:** your notes say the Sultan surrendered sovereignty "by 1950" and the Sabah incursion was in "2012." Standard dates: the **Carpenter Agreement, 1915**, and the **Lahad Datu standoff, 2013**. Ask what Sir said.

### Organic laws and structure

| Law | Year | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Philippine Bill (Cooper Act)** | **July 1, 1902** | Basic law for **14 years**; a **transitional constitution** built like a constitution. Had a **bill of rights** (speech, religion). **Sovereignty followed the US flag, but not the Constitution.** Local courts (Courts of First Instance) but **no Court of Appeals**: the **US Supreme Court** was the final court, and the PH Supreme Court acted like an appeals court. |

## Topic 3.5: The birth of the mestizo (Agabin) \[NEEDS VERIFICATION\]

**In plain words:** our legal system is a mix. Spain gave us the civil codes for family, property, and contracts. America added the Constitution, police power, due process, court procedure, and commercial law. That blend is what Agabin calls "mestizo." It's about legal mixing, not race.

**Main thesis:** **Spanish civil law + American common law + local/customary law = mestizo legal system.**

### Historical background

- **Feb. 15, 1898:** USS Maine explodes in Havana → catalyst for the Spanish-American War (April 1898).
- **May 1, 1898:** Battle of Manila Bay; **Admiral George Dewey** defeats the Spanish fleet.
- **Manifest Destiny:** American expansionism framed as civilization, education, uplift, Christianization.
- **Dec. 10, 1898:** **Treaty of Paris**: Spain cedes the PH to the US for **$20 million**.
- **Feb. 1899:** Philippine-American War.
- The **US War Department** (Secretary **Elihu Root**) ran PH affairs.
- **Sir's point:** the takeover was not just military; it became a **legal and institutional transformation**.

### 3 levels of American penetration

1. **Political:** occupation → annexation → colonial government.
2. **Institutional:** reorganized government, courts, procedure, legislation, administration.
3. **Jurisprudential:** reshaped due process, police power, liberty of contract, precedent, judicial interpretation.

**Conquest → institutionalization → legal hybridization.**

### What Spain left vs. what America added (Sir's lecture)

| Spanish changes | American changes |
| --- | --- |
| **Código Civil:** property and economics (private property); family and faith | **Politics → constitutionalism:** the Constitution as supreme law: **imperious, absolute, unalterable, supreme** |
| **Código de Comercio** | **Control:** the hegemony of **police power** and regulation |
| **Código Penal:** accountability and liability | **Property:** no monarch, so who owns everything? **The State.** Plus **self-protection** (title; right to protect your property from intrusion) and **social function** (freedom, but not at the expense of others) |
| Law is still mostly understood the way Spain understood it | **Due process:** violence is no longer private; the **State** uses it through the courts |

- **Fun fact:** the **easement of light and view** lets you act against a structure that blocks your window's light.

> **Check:** The Spanish/American legal divide table (Código Civil/Comercio/Penal vs. constitutionalism/police power/due process) and the "easement of light and view" example are Sir's lecture framing.

### Constitutionalism and the colonial contradiction

- Americans said Filipinos needed to be civilized, educated, and prepared for self-government by American constitutional standards (rule of law, liberty, good government).
- **Contradiction:** teaching constitutional government while keeping colonial sovereignty.
- **Constitutional supremacy:** Constitution → statutes → administrative rules. A lower law can't override the Constitution. **Government itself is subject to law.**
- **Does the US Constitution "follow the flag"?** **Not automatically, and not entirely** (the **Insular Cases**): the PH was an **unincorporated territory**. The **Organic Act of 1902** made inhabitants **"citizens of the Philippine Islands,"** not US citizens. This affected constitutional rights, taxation, political rights.
- **Policy of attraction:** **Taft** used **ilustrados** (lawyers, doctors, professionals, landowners) as intermediaries. **Partido Federalista** favored closer US integration, even statehood.
- The **US Supreme Court** had appellate jurisdiction over PH cases, so PH courts relied on **American precedents**. The 1900 **Philippine Commission** had legislative and executive powers; Americans dominated the judiciary.

### Police power and property

- **Police power** = the State's power to regulate private conduct, property, and business for **public welfare and self-protection**. Three ideas: **self-protection, state authority, social function.**
- **Private rights are not absolute.** Property can be regulated when its use harms others, affects public welfare, creates a nuisance, or threatens safety.
- **Property → social function → police power → regulation.**
- **De Villata v. Stanley:** shipping companies were required to carry government mail. The Court first upheld it (entering a regulated business means accepting conditions); the **US Supreme Court reversed** it under due process. Shows the tension between **police power** and **due process / property rights**.

### Due process

| Procedural due process | Substantive due process |
| --- | --- |
| Was the correct **procedure** followed? | Is the government **action itself** allowed? Even with proper procedure, it can be unconstitutional if it interferes too much with liberty or property. |

**Development:** early period → police power emphasized → **1920s** → substantive due process → liberty of contract → laissez-faire → stronger protection of property and economic liberty.

### Procedure: inquisitorial → adversarial

| Spanish: inquisitorial | American: accusatorial / adversarial |
| --- | --- |
| The court has a strong investigative role | Prosecution and defense argue before a **neutral** court |

- **General Order No. 68:** ended exclusive **church control over marriage**; recognized **civil marriage** (connected to the old Siete Partidas framework).
- **General Order No. 58 (April 23, 1900):** new **Code of Criminal Procedure**; **inquisitorial → accusatorial**; aimed to protect civil liberties.
- The **Philippine Commission** passed a large body of public law: municipal and provincial government, Manila, elections, Baguio, Mindanao and Sulu, courts, civil procedure.

### Jurisprudence, equity, and common law

- **Jurisprudence = juris** (law, rights) + **prudence** (careful judgment). Court → decision → legal reasoning → jurisprudence → **precedent** → **binding effect**. A decision can have the **"force and effect of law."** Common law is not "judges making random laws."

| Courts of law | Courts of equity |
| --- | --- |
| Apply established legal rules | Find the **appropriate solution with respect to the rights involved**: fairness, remedies, balancing rights |

Over time, law and equity **merged**. (Galindez: common law used to be just courts of equity; when merged with courts of law, law and rights were combined.)

| Civil law | Common law |
| --- | --- |
| Codes and statutes from parliament/legislature | Judicial decisions and precedent from courts |
| Spanish foundation | Anglo-American influence |

We are a **mix of both**, and the two can say completely different things.

### Illegally obtained evidence

**Spanish:** generally admissible → **early American** (Adams v. New York tradition): still admissible → later PH developments → eventually the **exclusionary rule**. Americanization happened in steps, not all at once.

### Retention of private and customary law

- Americanization did **not** replace everything. The **Spanish Civil Code of 1889** stayed important for family, property, contracts, succession, personal status.
- The **Spanish Penal Code of 1870** stayed foundational and became the basis of the **Revised Penal Code**.
- **Customary law** continued where state penetration was incomplete: **Muslim Mindanao, highland communities, ancestral domains**.

### American common-law doctrines inserted into Spanish law

| Area | Spanish base | American additions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Torts / quasi-delicts** | Spanish structure | Proximate cause, contributory negligence, last clear chance, vicarious liability, sovereign immunity |
| **Contracts** | **Causa** | **Consideration** (both now part of PH contract law) |
| **Property** | Movable / immovable | Land / personal property, equity, trusts |
| **Succession** | Spanish framework | Heir, devisee, legatee |

**Commercial law = the heaviest Americanization:** Corporation Law (Act No. 1459), Negotiable Instruments Law (Act No. 2031), Insolvency Act (Act No. 1956), Warehouse Receipts Law (Act No. 2137), Insurance Law (Act No. 2427), public utilities laws.

### The mestizo legal system

| Spanish foundation | American overlay | Local/customary layer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civil law, family, property, contracts, succession, much criminal law | Constitutional and public law, judicial review, procedure, commercial law, precedent, due process, police power, common-law reasoning | Survived where state penetration was incomplete |

**"Mestizo" is a metaphor for legal hybridization**, not racial mixing.

| --- | --- |

- Responds to excessive centralization: authority is **constitutionally divided** between national and regional/state governments.
- **Dual sovereignty:** both levels have constitutionally protected spheres.
- **Subsidiarity:** handle public matters at the **lowest capable level**.

### Executive systems

**Presidential:** President = head of state + head of government; President and legislature separately elected; fixed term.

- **Gridlock / dual legitimacy:** both President and legislature claim a popular mandate → conflict.
- **Personality and patronage politics:** celebrity, wealth, media, family names, weak parties, **turncoatism**, patronage, pork barrel.
- **Rigidity:** a fixed term makes changing the head of government hard outside impeachment or crisis.

**Parliamentary:** the Prime Minister heads the government and comes from the parliamentary majority. President/Monarch = head of state.

- **Fusion of powers:** legislature and executive are connected (electorate → assembly → cabinet headed by PM).
- Politics centers on **parties and programs**, not personalities and popularity. A politician rises through the party to become party leader.
- **Vote of no confidence:** parliament withdraws support → government replaced or new elections. A political **safety valve**.
- **Constructive vote of confidence (Germany):** parliament **removes and replaces** the PM at the same time. Memory: **REMOVE + REPLACE**.
- **Weakness: immobilism.** No majority or a fragmented party system → unstable government, **revolving PMs**. Examples: **France's Fourth Republic, Italy, post-Koizumi Japan.**

**Abueva's 8 advantages of parliamentary government:**

1. **Coordination:** fused executive and legislature.
2. **Prime Minister** emerges from parliament.
3. **Political parties** become democratic, disciplined, united, effective.
4. **Timely change of government** via no-confidence vote.
5. **Government program** shaped by the majority party.
6. **Party choice:** voters choose a party, not just candidates.
7. **Lower election cost:** no separate nationwide race for head of government.
8. **Less personality-based politics:** less emphasis on wealth, popularity, name recall.

**Semi-presidential (dual executive):** a **popularly elected President** (head of state, often defense and foreign affairs) + a **Prime Minister** accountable to the parliamentary majority (head of government, day-to-day). Same camp → easy coordination; opposing camps → conflict.

| System | Head of state | Head of government | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Presidential | President | President | Gridlock, personality politics, rigidity |
| Parliamentary | Monarch / President | **Prime Minister** | Immobilism, unstable government |
| Semi-presidential | Elected President | Prime Minister | President-PM conflict |

| **National security** | Defense + internal order/law enforcement. The **National Security Council** sits directly below the President as an intermediary body. The **military** bureaucracy is separate; the armed forces don't go to department secretaries for clearance. |
| **Law enforcement** | **General:** PNP. **Specialized:** SAF, Coast Guard |
| **Regional** | **Autonomous regions** (Bangsamoro) and **administrative regions** (because of Mindanao and the Cordillera) |
| **Intergovernmental** | Bureaucracies that link levels of government |

**All these bureaucracies are run by the President.** That's the Philippine pangulo regime.

> **Check:** The functional breakdown (each branch, independent bodies, state finance fiscal/monetary, public corporations LGUs/GOCCs, national security + military separate, law enforcement PNP/SAF/Coast Guard, regional autonomous/administrative, intergovernmental) — all under presidential prerogative — is Sir's lecture punchline.

### Agpalo's pangulo regime

| Regime | Origin | Supremacy | Core value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Parliamentary** (Westminster) | UK | **Legislature** ("parliamentary sovereignty") | **Liberty** (democracy) |
| **Presidential** | US | **Balance**: separation of powers, executive equal to legislative | **Equality** |
| **Pangulo** | PH | **Executive** | **Fraternity / pagdamay** |

**Why pagdamay?** Under Spain we lacked **liberty**; under America we faced **inequality**. We fought for freedom, then independence and equality, yet our regime centered on **fraternity**. Pagdamay runs through **La Propaganda** (people voluntarily funded **La Solidaridad**), **La Liga**, and **Rizal**. It was a **core value of the First Philippine Republic**.

**The Malolos puzzle:** if Malolos emphasized legislative supremacy, how did the First Republic become a pangulo regime?

1. **On paper, legislative supremacy:** the Malolos Constitution set three powers and put legislative power in the **Assembly of Representatives (Art. 33)**. The **President was elected by the Assembly**, not the people. Designed by **Felipe Calderón** as parliamentary.
2. **In practice (Agpalo):** the **transitory provisions** changed how it worked. **Art. 99** let the government, while Congress was closed during the independence war, **resolve unforeseen questions by decree**.
3. So **Agpalo calls Aguinaldo's First Republic a "true and real pangulo regime."** After the Treaty of Paris and the Philippine-American War, Aguinaldo as "dictator" held legislative power as a **transitory** measure, since no legislature could be appointed yet. He went from dictator to president.

**Presidents and legislative power:**

| President | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| **Aguinaldo** | Dictator, then President; took legislative power as **transitory** |
| **Marcos** | **Usurped** legislative power entirely |
| **Corazon Aquino** | 1986 Freedom Constitution; laws issued as **executive orders**, e.g., the **Administrative Code (EO 292)** and the **Family Code**. Like Aguinaldo's, her decrees were **transitory**. |
| **Gloria Arroyo** | Declared a **state of national emergency**, using emergency powers. Questioned in court. **SC:** the President may declare it, but **only Congress can grant the exercise of emergency powers**. Seen as an attempt to create a regime. |

> **Check:** Galindez wrote that the SC said Arroyo "can declare martial law." Your 4.1 notes say **state of national emergency**. Go with your notes, but confirm.

**Regime vs. model of a regime:**

- A **regime** is **institutionalized**: a **constitution**, an **ongoing government**, **national symbols**, **armed forces**, **national anthem**, and other paraphernalia (e.g., a national university).
- Later presidents didn't build regimes; they **copied Quezon's model**. Philippine presidents give us a **model** of a pangulo regime.

### The Vice President

- **Can the VP be appointed to another office?** Yes. The PH VP can be appointed to any office or department and do real work.
- In the US, the VP is a ceremonial **"spare tire"** and the butt of jokes. The PH has used the VP's office to its full potential.
- The VP is a **fiscalizing** office, an **institutional evolution**, and can be a force for good or bad.
- Even without set functions, the VP can be very useful. What the VP does depends on the **President's discretion**.

### Residual powers and assuming office

- **Marcos v. Manglapus (1989):** Marcos, in Hawaii for 3 years, petitioned (mandamus) to return. **Denied for national security**: he'd be a hero to loyalists who wouldn't let go of power, his return would cause unrest between loyalists and People Power supporters, and there were plans to stage a coup against Cory.
- **Residual powers:** the President holds powers from the **entire democratic mandate**, even if not listed in the Constitution. These were part of why Marcos was barred.
- **Assuming office:** the SC said **Arroyo** assumed office through the **direct exercise of democracy**, recognized by the sovereign Filipino people. **Cory** assumed office through **revolution**.
- The President is like an **edge case**: the only real bound is the **Bill of Rights**.

### Immunity from suit

- You **can't sue the President during incumbency**. Origin: the old idea that "the king can do no wrong" (Galindez: a bit contradictory).
- Suits are **deferred**, not erased; they can pile up ("pwedeng maipon").
- Covers **liabilities only**, not accountability.
- **Workaround:** file the case against the **Executive Secretary**.
- **Accountability remedies still available:** **habeas corpus, amparo, habeas data.**

### The President as a symbol above politics

- **Duterte** became a symbol **above politics**, still popular in **May 2025**; he "transcended from a person to a deity" (**political anthropology**).
- Under the **Boy Scouts' organic law**, the President is the **Chief Scout**.
- The President is supposed to be the **head of state**, so the President appointing a justice or members of the Constitutional Commissions is **not** a violation of separation of powers.

### Why "hyper" pangulo

**Many bureaucracies + all under the President + executive supremacy rooted in pagdamay + emergency and residual powers + immunity + symbolic status + appointing power over co-equal bodies = "madaming butas, kaya hyper pangulo."**

> **Check:** V2 synthesis — the same presidential strength that coordinates bureaucracy, responds to emergencies, directs national policy, and influences legislation also creates risks of overcentralization, patronage, abuse of authority, and authoritarianism. The 1987 Constitution combines executive capacity with safeguards. **Pangulo regime = executive supremacy in the Philippine experience.** The lecture's blunt summary: **"Madaming butas kaya hyper pangulo."**

The Philippine Presidency and Executive Bureaucracy

- As a leader for national survival, the President has significant powers to respond to threats to national security, public safety, and the national economy.
  - Congress may authorize the President, by law, to exercise powers necessary and proper to carry out a declared national policy during war or another national emergency. The delegation must be subject to restrictions prescribed by Congress and may be withdrawn by resolution.
  - As Commander-in-Chief, the President is authorized to call out the armed forces to prevent or suppress lawless violence, invasion, or rebellion. In cases of invasion or rebellion, when public safety requires it, the President may suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus or place the Philippines, or any part of it, under martial law for a period not exceeding 60 days, subject to congressional review and judicial review.
  - The State, during a national emergency and when the public interest requires, may temporarily take over or direct the operation of privately owned public utilities or businesses affected with public interest.
- As an economic leader, the President:
  - may contract or guarantee foreign loans on behalf of the Republic, subject to prior concurrence of the Monetary Board and statutory limitations.
  - required to submit the proposed national budget to Congress within 30 days from the opening of every regular session.
  - may veto particular items in an appropriation, revenue, or tariff bill without vetoing the entire bill.
  - may exercise tariff powers delegated by Congress, subject to statutory limits.
  - may enter into treaties or international agreements, subject to the constitutional requirement of concurrence by at least two-thirds of all the Members of the Senate.
  - implements this constitutional policy on natural resources through administrative agencies and through constitutionally permitted agreements and dispositions.
  - appoints members of the Monetary Board.
- As a legislative gatekeeper, the President participates in legislation through:
  - General veto power. Every bill passed by Congress is presented to the President. The President may approve it or veto it. A veto may be overridden by a vote of two-thirds of all the Members of the House of Representatives and two-thirds of all the Members of the Senate, voting separately.
  - Line-item veto power. The President may veto one or more items in an appropriation, revenue, or tariff bill, while approving the other items.
  - Budget control. The President proposes which expenditure items will be included in the national budget and administers appropriations after enactment.
  - Legislative agenda. The President shapes the legislative agenda through the State of the Nation Address.
- Control means the power to alter, modify, nullify, or set aside what a subordinate officer has done in the performance of official duties and to substitute the President’s judgment for that of the subordinate.
  - It does not depend on statutory implementation and cannot be withdrawn or substantially limited by ordinary legislation.
  - It extends to executive departments, bureaus, offices, and executive officers, including officers appointed by subordinate bodies.
  - When a law entrusts a function to an executive officer, the President may personally perform or direct the performance of that function, unless the Constitution or law requires personal presidential action.
- Under the doctrine of qualified political agency, department secretaries are the President’s alter egos in matters within their departments. Their acts, when performed within the scope of authority and not disapproved or reprobated by the President, are presumptively acts of the President.
  - The President retains the authority to confirm, modify, or reverse acts taken by department secretaries.
  - The President cannot delegate functions that the Constitution or law requires the President to exercise personally.
  - The President also cannot control independent constitutional commissions in the performance of their constitutionally protected functions.
- The President’s reorganization authority is related to, but distinct from, the power of control. While control concerns the revision of subordinate acts, reorganization is concerned with the structure, location, powers, and functions of executive offices.
  - P.D. No. 1416 (1978) grants continuing authority to reorganize the administrative structure of the National Government;
  - P.D. No. 1772 (1981), amending P.D. No. 1416, expanded the authority to create, abolish, merge, or modify government offices and government-owned or controlled corporations; and
- The authority of the President to reorganize includes:
  - transferring offices or functions;
  - consolidating or merging offices;
  - abolishing offices;
  - creating offices where legally authorized;
  - transferring personnel and resources; and
  - realigning the administrative structure of the Executive branch.
- Reorganization is valid only when supported by the Constitution, statute, or a valid continuing authority. It cannot be used as a device to remove a public officer in violation of security of tenure, nor may it alter offices or functions beyond the authority granted by law. An example of the legal authorization is the Government Optimization Act.
- The President has the power to appoint:
  - heads of executive departments (i.e. Secretaries or Chairpersons;
  - ambassadors and other public ministers and consuls;
  - officers of the armed forces from the rank of colonel or naval captain;
  - other officers whose appointments are vested in the President by the Constitution; and
  - officers whose appointments are not otherwise provided by law.
- The President also appoints other officers as may be provided by law, subject to the constitutional requirement of Commission on Appointments confirmation where applicable.
- Midnight appointments are generally prohibited from two months before the next presidential election until the end of the President’s term, except for appointments to the Judiciary, which have to be filled under a strict timeline and temporary appointments to executive positions when continued vacancies would prejudice public service or endanger public safety.
- The President may make temporary appointments during legislative recess, subject to the constitutional rules on ad interim appointments.
- The Constitution allows the President, Senate President, Speaker of the House, Chief Justice, and heads of constitutional commissions to augment an item in the General Appropriations Act for their respective offices from savings in other items of their appropriations.

### Final Jeopardy

#### Final Jeopardy 1 — The Hyper Pangulo Formula: Law, History, and Informal Institutions
How do Spanish colonial centralization, the American gendarmerie model (Philippine Constabulary), the value of pagdamay, and the 1987 constitutional architecture together explain why the Philippine Presidency is described as a 'Hyper Pangulo'?

##### Answer
The Philippine presidency combines: (1) Spanish colonial executive centralization (Governor-General holding civil, military, and judicial power); (2) American institutional militarization (the Constabulary gendarmerie making the executive the default response to civil, health, and disaster emergencies); (3) cultural legitimacy rooted in pagdamay (fraternal executive leadership caring for the people); and (4) the 1987 constitutional design where, despite extensive fail-safes, the President retains sweeping control over all executive bureaucracies, appointments, budget proposals, and residual powers—leading to the lecture's conclusion that 'madaming butas kaya hyper pangulo.'

#### Final Jeopardy 2 — Formal Rules vs. Informal Institutions in Administrative Reform
Drawing from Casis's 'fused society,' Tiglao's 'decapitalization,' and Cleofas's analysis of utang na loob, explain why formal administrative reforms imported from Western liberal democracies repeatedly get subverted in the Philippine bureaucracy.

##### Answer
Western models assume strict separation of powers, impersonal Weberian rule enforcement, and independent market institutions. In the Philippines: (1) Casis shows that our indigenous foundation was a fused society where moral, political, and economic authority resided in single leaders, making Western institutional separation unnatural; (2) Tiglao demonstrates that formal legal barriers (like the 60% Filipino ownership rule) are easily bypassed by informal financial engineering (voting preferred shares); and (3) Cleofas explains that informal relational obligations (utang na loob, pakikisama) fill the vacuum left by weak state services, turning public office into personal patronage unless actively regulated by katarungan (justice).

#### Final Jeopardy 3 — The Constitutional Dilemma of Philippine Decentralization and Autonomy
Contrast the historical and constitutional outcomes of the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) and the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM). Why does administrative recognition not equal genuine constitutional autonomy?

##### Answer
CAR remains an administrative region created by executive order (EO 220) after autonomy plebiscites failed in 1990 and 1998 due to inter-tribal distrust, leaving it dependent on national line agencies. In contrast, BARMM achieved genuine constitutional autonomy through a decades-long historical chain (land dispossession → armed rebellion → peace accords → Bangsamoro Organic Law), establishing an asymmetric parliamentary government with legislative powers and fiscal block grants. This demonstrates that administrative decentralization merely disperses central bureaucratic tasks, whereas genuine autonomy requires constitutionally divided power and fiscal self-determination.
